Chapter 8: Deal Structure and Transaction Mechanics – How Deals Are Built and Closed

Why Deal Structure Matters

Two buyers are competing for the same 200 MW wind project. Both offer the same price. One structures the deal as an asset purchase; the other as an equity purchase. The seller accepts the equity offer—not because the price is higher, but because the equity structure avoids triggering a tax event for the seller and does not require third-party consents for contract assignment.

Deal structure is not administrative paperwork. It determines who bears which risks, who gets what tax treatment, and whether the transaction can close at all.

This chapter explains the primary M&A deal structures used in renewable energy transactions, the key transaction documents, and the mechanics of moving from letter of intent to closing.

Asset Purchase vs. Equity Purchase

The most fundamental structural decision in renewable energy M&A is whether to buy the project’s assets or the equity of the entity that owns the project.

Asset Purchase

In an asset purchase, the buyer acquires specific assets—the land lease, the PPA, the interconnection agreement, the equipment—directly from the seller entity.

Buyer advantages: - Buyer does not inherit the seller’s liabilities (except those specifically assumed). - Buyer gets a stepped-up tax basis in the acquired assets, enabling higher depreciation deductions. - Buyer can be selective about which contracts and liabilities to assume.

Buyer disadvantages: - Most contracts (PPA, interconnection, land lease) require counterparty consent to assign. Obtaining consents takes time and may be refused. - Asset transfers trigger transfer taxes in many states. - More complex to execute—each asset transfer requires separate documentation.

Equity Purchase

In an equity purchase, the buyer acquires the membership interests or shares of the entity that owns the project. The entity—and all its assets and liabilities—transfers to the buyer intact.

Buyer advantages: - Most contracts transfer automatically with the entity—no counterparty consent needed (unless the contract includes change-of-control provisions). - Cleaner execution—one transfer of equity rather than dozens of asset transfers. - Preferred structure for projects with complex tax equity arrangements.

Buyer disadvantages: - Buyer inherits all liabilities of the entity, including unknown or contingent liabilities. - No step-up in tax basis—the buyer inherits the seller’s depreciation history. - Seller may face different (potentially more favorable) tax treatment on equity sales.

Transaction Structure Comparison

The Transaction Timeline

Renewable energy M&A transactions typically follow a structured sequence from initial interest to closing.

Phase 1: Origination and Indication of Interest (IOI)

  • Buyer identifies target; seller accepts or initiates process.
  • Non-binding indication of interest submitted.
  • Preliminary financial model prepared.
  • Non-disclosure agreement (NDA) executed.

Phase 2: Letter of Intent (LOI)

  • Non-binding term sheet outlining price, structure, key conditions.
  • Exclusivity period negotiated (typically 60–120 days).
  • Initial due diligence begins on permitted information.
  • Buyer engages counsel, Independent Engineer, and environmental consultants.

Phase 3: Due Diligence

  • Full access to data room.
  • Review of all project documents: land agreements, permits, PPA, interconnection, tax equity, O&M contracts.
  • Site visit and Independent Engineer report.
  • Environmental site assessments.
  • Title review and survey.
  • Legal, tax, and financial model review.

Phase 4: Purchase Agreement Negotiation

  • Definitive Purchase and Sale Agreement (PSA) drafted and negotiated.
  • Representations and warranties negotiated.
  • Indemnification provisions finalized.
  • Closing conditions identified.
  • Representations and warranties insurance (RWI) arranged if applicable.

Phase 5: Closing

  • All closing conditions satisfied.
  • Third-party consents obtained.
  • Financing funded.
  • Transfer documents executed and delivered.
  • Purchase price paid.

Phase 6: Post-Closing

  • Transfer filings with regulatory agencies.
  • Permit transfers completed.
  • Tax returns and K-1s coordinated with tax equity investors.
  • Transition services (if any) completed.

Key Transaction Documents

Purchase and Sale Agreement (PSA)

The PSA is the central transaction document. Key provisions include:

Representations and Warranties: Seller statements about the state of the project—title, permits, contracts, financial condition, absence of litigation. Breaches of reps and warranties trigger indemnification obligations.

Covenants: Obligations of each party between signing and closing. Sellers typically covenant to operate the project in the ordinary course and not enter material new contracts without buyer consent.

Closing Conditions: Events that must occur before either party is obligated to close—receipt of third-party consents, absence of material adverse change, regulatory approvals.

Indemnification: The mechanism for compensating a party for losses arising from the other party’s breach. Indemnification provisions typically include a basket (minimum threshold), a cap (maximum liability), and a survival period.

Purchase Price Adjustments: Mechanisms to adjust the purchase price at closing for changes in working capital, debt, or other agreed metrics between signing and closing.

Representations and Warranties Insurance (RWI)

RWI has become standard in renewable energy M&A. RWI provides insurance coverage for losses arising from breaches of seller reps and warranties. Key features:

  • Eliminates or reduces the seller indemnity (seller can take proceeds at closing)
  • Coverage amounts typically equal to 10–15% of deal value
  • Retention (deductible) typically 1% of enterprise value
  • 3-year survival for general reps; 6-year for fundamental reps; full statute of limitations for fraud

Earn-Out Structures

When buyers and sellers disagree on value—often because a project’s permitting or financing is incomplete—earn-out structures bridge the gap:

Construction Earn-Out: Buyer pays a base price at closing, with additional payments if the project achieves commercial operation within a specified timeframe.

Performance Earn-Out: Additional payments triggered when the project achieves specified energy production milestones.

Incentive Earn-Out: Additional payments if the project qualifies for specified tax incentives (ITC, PTC) at rates the buyer models but the seller cannot guarantee.

Earn-outs create post-closing disputes when the triggering conditions are not clearly defined. Draft earn-out provisions with precision.

Best Practices for Transaction Structuring

  1. Decide on Asset vs. Equity Early: The structure decision affects due diligence scope, tax modeling, and closing documentation. Decide in the LOI phase, not the PSA phase.

  2. Map All Third-Party Consents: Before signing, identify every contract that requires counterparty consent and assess the risk of refusal or delay.

  3. Negotiate a Clean Closing Condition: Include a closing condition requiring no material adverse change in project status—permits, contracts, financing—between signing and closing.

  4. Consider RWI: Representations and warranties insurance is now available and affordable for most transactions above $20M in value. The elimination of seller indemnity often allows sellers to distribute proceeds immediately.

  5. Build Realistic Timelines: Renewable energy M&A transactions take 3–6 months from LOI to close. Environmental surveys, interconnection confirmations, and tax equity consents all take time.

Key Takeaways

  • Asset purchases provide tax benefits and liability protection but require contract consent. Equity purchases are cleaner but carry legacy liabilities.
  • Renewable energy M&A follows a structured six-phase process from origination to post-closing integration.
  • The PSA’s representations and warranties are the primary risk allocation mechanism—negotiate them carefully.
  • Representations and warranties insurance has transformed the indemnification landscape in renewable energy M&A.
  • Earn-outs are a useful bridge tool but create post-closing disputes when imprecisely drafted.
