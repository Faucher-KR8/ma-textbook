Chapter 4: Unseen Challenges – Navigating Mineral Rights

You can’t control the sun or the wind, but you can control your mineral risk.

When acquiring land, it is important to understand that there are surface rights and there are severed rights — rights separate from surface ownership. Mineral rights refer to ownership of underground minerals; a subsurface severance refers to minerals legally separated from the surface estate. Because mineral rights laws vary significantly by jurisdiction, developers should consult qualified legal counsel and title professionals regarding project-specific risks and compliance obligations.

In many states, mineral rights can be separated from surface rights, allowing different owners for underground resources and the land above. The mineral owner typically holds the dominant estate — the right to access the surface to extract minerals.

Tribal Mineral Rights Complexity

On tribal lands, the federal government may own the subsurface estate while private individuals or tribes own the surface — often set for a fixed term (a term mineral interest), after which rights return to the surface owner. This is most common in Texas and other states with complex oil and gas law. Mineral rights on tribal lands may involve separate federal oversight or distinct tribal governance requirements; surface and subsurface rights are not always aligned, and additional approvals may be required before development can proceed. Developers should approach tribal projects as long-term government-to-government relationships rather than conventional land acquisitions — success depends as much on trust, transparency, and cultural understanding as on contractual structure.

From Severance to Curative: Why Mineral Rights Demand Specialized Due Diligence

When minerals are severed from the surface estate, the property has **two chains of title**: one for the surface, one for the subsurface. While the surface chain can usually be reviewed by a title company, the subsurface requires a specialized search known as a **Mineral Ownership Report (MOR)**. Unlike a standard title commitment, an MOR often traces ownership back to the 1800s. Heirs may inherit rights they don't know exist; leases are forgotten; rights go unexercised until energy prices make them valuable. Developers can encounter "breaks" in the chain where ownership is unclear or fragmented across generations. For M&A due diligence, the implications are profound: - **Time** — these searches can take months, making early detection critical. - **Cost** — curative work to resolve gaps, probate issues, or missing heirs can run high. - **Insurability** — subsurface rights must be affirmatively insured just like surface rights, or deals risk collapse at closing. ::: {.callout-important} **Key M&A Takeaway:** If a potential severance is flagged, order MORs immediately. Ignoring subsurface rights until late in the deal often results in closing delays, renegotiations, or failed transactions. ::: ## Implications for Renewable Energy Projects - **Surface Disruption:** Mineral rights holders are typically entitled to access and use the surface for extraction. - **Operational Risks:** Drilling or mining may disrupt renewable facility operations — and this risk does not disappear simply because production is inactive at acquisition. Existing mineral owners may retain rights to future access, pipelines, or production infrastructure that could interfere with operations years or decades later. - **Insurance Limitations:** Title insurance policies may exclude mineral rights issues, increasing risk. The government reserves mineral rights on land grant deeds west of the Mississippi, often superseding private ownership — developers should examine deed specifics and government reservations carefully. Dormant vs. Producing Mineral Rights

One of the most important questions developers should ask in early diligence: “Has there ever been production on this property, or even an attempt to produce?”

Dormant mineral rights may appear harmless because no active drilling is taking place — but dormant does not mean extinguished. Ownership records may be incomplete, heirs may be impossible to locate easily, and interests may be divided among many descendants. Reconstructing those chains can require probate review, heirship verification, and historical title analysis stretching back generations. The risk here is uncertainty — not knowing who owns the rights or whether claims may surface later.

Producing mineral rights create a different exposure. Ownership is often easier to trace because operators have already researched the estate, but active production introduces operational conflicts: in many jurisdictions the mineral estate is dominant over the surface estate, so operators may retain broad rights to access and use the surface for extraction — directly affecting solar arrays, turbine placement, battery storage, access roads, and collector systems. Historical production from abandoned or capped wells may carry lingering legal issues if leases were never formally terminated; active production typically requires negotiated surface use waivers, subordination agreements, or non-disturbance arrangements; exploration leases mean operators retain the right to initiate future activity.

Comprehensive due diligence should review production history, lease activity, operator records, and oil and gas databases, and determine whether surface use agreements exist or permit filings suggest future development interest. These findings directly influence valuation, underwriting, financing terms, and construction planning. Title insurers evaluate active and historical mineral activity more closely than dormant estates, and underwriters may require recorded surface use waivers or subordinations — or exclude mineral-related risk from coverage entirely if adequate protection can’t be secured.

Timing is critical. Mineral ownership research, negotiations with operators, and curative work can take months. Projects that identify these risks early retain far greater flexibility to redesign layouts, negotiate agreements, adjust valuation assumptions, or sequence financing. Proactively approached operators can become valuable partners in resolving mineral risk rather than obstacles to development.

Mineral Rights Are a Bankability Trigger

Mineral rights are not simply a title issue — they are a bankability issue. Lenders, tax equity investors, and future acquirers increasingly require developers to identify and mitigate mineral risks before financing can proceed. Standard title insurance often excludes mineral-related risk unless specialized endorsements, curative actions, or a separate mineral ownership analysis are obtained. Even balance-sheet-financed projects may face future transaction challenges if mineral exposure remains unresolved.

Case Study: Rethinking Mineral Surface Waiver Requirements Submitted by Senior Title Insurance Expert Tara Brown-Selders

A battery energy storage system (BESS) project in Texas presented an opportunity to evaluate mineral risk differently than traditional solar development. For solar projects, developers typically obtain surface waivers from 100% of mineral owners to prevent drilling from interfering with arrays and infrastructure. This battery storage site consisted of roughly thirty acres, but the equipment itself would occupy only ten acres.

Risk: The standard approach would have required surface waivers from every mineral interest holder across the entire property — costly and time-consuming for a project using only a third of the land.

Strategy: The developer, relying on a trusted title insurance sales representative, proposed obtaining waivers only across the portion of the property where the battery storage equipment would actually sit. Multiple conversations reviewed the site plan and the acreage that would remain available if mineral development ever occurred. The team evaluated the issue through a project-specific lens rather than defaulting to standard practice.

Outcome: Underwriting agreed that surface waivers needed to cover only about 30% of the property rather than the full acreage. The project moved forward with appropriate title coverage while preserving operational flexibility across the rest of the land — avoiding unnecessary time and expense.

Key Takeaway: New project types and land-use patterns call for project-specific mineral risk analysis rather than blanket application of solar-industry norms. An experienced title professional who understands both development and underwriting perspectives can bridge the two and unlock practical solutions.

Mineral Rights Due Diligence Checklist

Preliminary Review

  • Order Mineral Ownership Report (MOR)
  • Review title commitments
  • Identify active production
  • Review spacing orders
  • Review pooling / unitization orders

State-Specific Review

  • Dormant mineral laws
  • Disclosure laws
  • Dominant estate rules

Financing Review

  • ALTA 35.1 endorsement availability
  • Title carve-outs
  • Lender requirements

Operational Review

  • Drill islands
  • Access corridors
  • Pipeline conflicts
  • Setback impacts

Beyond Mineral Rights — Other Regional Risks

  • Water Rights: Especially critical in arid regions.
  • Environmental Regulations: Local laws may impose additional requirements.
  • Cultural Heritage Sites: Indigenous or historical landmarks can restrict development.

Real-World Example

A solar project in Texas was delayed when oil drilling commenced on the site due to pre-existing mineral rights not identified during due diligence. The developer entered negotiations with the mineral rights owner, resulting in a complete project redesign, significant delays, and increased costs.

Key Takeaways

  • Mineral rights create two parallel chains of title — surface and subsurface — and ignoring the subsurface chain is one of the most common causes of late-stage deal failure.
  • Order a Mineral Ownership Report (MOR) immediately whenever severance is suspected; the search can take months.
  • Dormant and producing mineral estates require fundamentally different mitigation strategies — uncertainty management versus active negotiation.
  • Mineral risk should be evaluated on a project-specific basis (footprint, technology type) rather than applying blanket industry norms.
  • Mineral exposure is a bankability issue: lenders and tax equity investors increasingly require it to be resolved before financing.
