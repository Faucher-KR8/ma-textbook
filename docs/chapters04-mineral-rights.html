## Why Mineral Rights Matter

A solar developer leases a 500-acre parcel in rural Texas. The lease is clear. The title is clean. The survey is precise. The buyer is ready to close.

Three days before closing, a title search reveals that subsurface mineral rights—the right to extract oil, gas, minerals, or metals from below the surface—have been severed from the land. A third party owns the mineral rights.

The third party contacts the developer: "We are beginning oil and gas operations on your property. You will need to accommodate our wells, access roads, and equipment."

The solar project is now in jeopardy. Equipment must be relocated. Visual impact is compromised. The project's bankability—its ability to secure financing—is in question.

This scenario plays out repeatedly in renewable energy M&A. **Mineral rights issues are among the most frequent causes of deal failure.**

This chapter explains what mineral rights are, how they create risk in renewable energy M&A, and how to identify and mitigate that risk.

## What Are Mineral Rights?

### Surface Rights vs. Mineral Rights

Land ownership comprises two components:

**Surface Rights:** The right to use the land surface for a specified purpose (agriculture, residential, commercial, renewable energy).

**Mineral Rights:** The right to extract minerals, metals, oil, and gas from below the surface.

Historically, surface rights and mineral rights were bundled together. A landowner owned both the surface and the minerals. Over time, particularly in oil and gas-rich regions, mineral rights were often severed from surface rights. A farmer might own the surface (allowing them to grow crops), while an oil company owns the minerals (allowing them to drill for oil below).

### Types of Mineral Interests

Mineral ownership varies widely:

**Owned Minerals:** The mineral rights owner has full title and control.

**Leased Minerals:** The mineral rights owner leases to an operator (oil, gas, or mining company) the right to extract for a specified term and royalty.

**Dormant or Abandoned Minerals:** The mineral rights owner is unknown, deceased, or inactive. The minerals have not been developed for decades.

**Overriding Royalty Interests (ORRIs):** A third party has a right to a percentage of revenues from mineral extraction, without bearing development costs.

## Mineral Rights and Renewable Energy Risk

Mineral rights create several categories of risk for renewable energy projects:

### 1. Surface Disturbance

If a third party controls mineral rights, they may claim the right to conduct operations on the surface in pursuit of mineral extraction—drilling wells, building access roads, installing pipelines, and constructing production facilities.

For a solar project, mineral operations create:

- **Physical conflict:** Wells, roads, or pipelines occupy surface area needed for solar arrays.
- **Visual impact:** Production facilities compromise the aesthetic of the project and surrounding lands.
- **Dust and noise:** Operations create environmental nuisances.
- **Safety risk:** Wells or pipelines pose safety and liability risks to project personnel and equipment.

### 2. Surface Use Agreement Disputes

Renewable projects typically require a "surface use agreement" between the surface rights holder (the developer) and the mineral rights holder. This agreement limits mineral operations to protect renewable assets.

Common disputes arise when:

- **Mineral rights holders claim broader surface rights than the surface use agreement permits.**
- **Renewable developers claim the surface use agreement is more restrictive than mineral rights holders' lease with their operator actually permits.**
- **An unexpected third party (an ORRI holder, a successor mineral lessee) emerges claiming rights not anticipated in the original surface use agreement.**

### 3. Title Insurance Exclusions

Title insurance typically excludes mineral rights. A title insurer will issue a standard exclusion for "mineral rights." This means:

- If mineral rights defects surface, the title insurer will not cover losses.
- The project owner bears the full risk of undisclosed mineral claims.

### 4. Financing Complications

Project lenders require clear title and minimal risk of surface disturbance. If mineral rights are severed or unclear, lenders may:

- **Refuse to finance** the project, or
- **Require a surface use agreement** as a condition of financing.

Many projects have been refinanced or restructured because mineral rights issues emerged post-acquisition.

8.3 Case Study: The Oil Lease Surprise

A developer acquired a 150 MW solar project in West Texas in 2019. Title was reported as “clear.” The buyer proceeded with financing and construction.

In 2022, an oil company contacted the developer: they had just acquired oil and gas rights beneath the project from a dormant mineral lessee. The oil company commenced surface activities—geological surveys and preparation for drilling.

The solar project’s performance was not affected, but the presence of oil operations created:

  1. Insurance complications: The project’s insurance carrier demanded higher premiums due to industrial activity nearby.
  2. Equipment access disputes: Access roads and laydown areas for O&M equipment conflicted with oil company access and equipment staging.
  3. Financing complications: When the project was refinanced three years later, lenders discovered the active mineral operations. Because the original due diligence had missed the minerals, the developer had no surface use agreement. Refinancing was delayed for six months pending negotiation of a surface use agreement.

The developer ultimately negotiated a surface use agreement limiting oil company operations, but at a cost of $1 million in annual payments to the mineral operator.

Lesson: Even if title appears clear, mineral rights must be separately investigated. Title insurance excludes mineral rights for this reason. The absence of a mineral exception in a title report does not mean the minerals are owned in fee with no restrictions.

8.4 Best Practices for Mineral Rights Diligence

  1. Investigate Early: Mineral rights investigation should begin during preliminary due diligence, before signing a purchase agreement.

  2. Use Specialists: Engage a title attorney and a state-law mineral rights expert. Their knowledge of dormant mineral act laws and mineral lease interpretation is invaluable.

  3. Get Details: Obtain copies of mineral leases, surface use agreements, and well records. Do not rely on summaries.

  4. Interview Neighbors: Ask local landowners, county officials, and operators about any mineral activity on or near the property.

  5. Negotiate Surface Use Agreements: If a surface use agreement does not exist, negotiate one before closing. Do not assume a mineral lessee will respect your project’s interests.

  6. Budget for Long-Term Risk: Even with due diligence, mineral risks can emerge years after acquisition. Budget for potential surface use agreement negotiations post-closing.

  7. Disclose Fully: If minerals are severed or unclear, disclose this to lenders and equity investors. Do not hope they will not ask.

8.5 Key Takeaways

  • Mineral rights—the right to extract oil, gas, or minerals from subsurface—are frequently severed from surface rights, particularly in oil and gas-rich states.
  • Severed mineral rights create risk of surface disturbance, conflicts with mineral operators, and title insurance exclusions.
  • High-risk states include Texas, Oklahoma, Colorado, Wyoming, and others with active oil and gas operations.
  • Mineral rights due diligence requires title review, mineral lease review, surface use agreement verification, and state-specific investigation.
  • Many M&A deals have been impaired post-closing because mineral rights were inadequately investigated pre-acquisition.
