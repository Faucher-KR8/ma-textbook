Chapter 13: Mitigating Renewable Infrastructure Risk with Nature-Based Solutions (NbS)

Nature-based solutions can help renewable energy developers reduce physical climate risk, support permitting, strengthen community acceptance, improve resilience, and potentially enhance financing and insurance outcomes. Once viewed mainly as environmental or philanthropic initiatives, NbS are increasingly recognized as practical infrastructure investments that protect asset value, reduce long-term risk, and improve project financeability. For developers, the question is no longer whether nature has value, but how to integrate that value into project design, risk management, and investment decisions.

NbS Are Becoming Relevant to M&A and Asset Valuation

Ecosystem resilience is increasingly recognized as a financial factor, not just an environmental one. Investors and acquirers are giving greater weight to flood exposure, biodiversity risk, erosion, and overall environmental resilience in project diligence and valuation.

NbS Can Improve Permitting and Community Acceptance

Many renewable projects face growing scrutiny over land use, biodiversity impacts, and environmental stewardship. Nature-based solutions can help address these concerns by integrating habitat restoration, watershed protection, agrivoltaics, and biodiversity enhancements into project design — in turn easing permitting, strengthening community relationships, and reducing opposition that can delay project schedules.

NbS May Help Lower Insurance and Financing Risk

Insurers are beginning to recognize the economic value of ecosystem protection because it can reduce future claims. As lenders, insurers, and investors place greater emphasis on climate resilience, projects that incorporate natural infrastructure to reduce risk may receive more favorable treatment during underwriting and diligence.

Real-World Example: Mangrove Restoration and Insurance Coverage

Mangrove restoration revitalizes degraded coastal ecosystems by repairing natural hydrology and planting native vegetation, recreating self-sustaining habitats that protect shorelines from erosion, provide marine nurseries, and capture carbon. As renewable energy infrastructure expands into climate-exposed regions, flooding, erosion, biodiversity impacts, and water-related disruptions can materially affect operations, permitting, insurance costs, and asset performance — making nature-based solutions relevant to both sustainability and infrastructure-finance diligence.

A key challenge is that NbS often create value through avoided losses rather than direct cash flow, which is difficult to capture in conventional project finance models. As a result, many NbS initiatives have historically relied on public or philanthropic capital despite delivering meaningful resilience benefits.

The insurance sector provides an early signal of how this gap may evolve. Tokio Marine Holdings, a leading Japanese insurer, has supported mangrove restoration projects in Southeast Asia since 1999 as part of a broader ecosystem-based adaptation strategy, responding to Japan’s exposure to coastal flooding, typhoons, and extreme weather.

Using ecosystem valuation approaches such as the Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity (TEEB) framework, Tokio Marine has reported approximately ¥12.6 billion in storm protection and damage reduction benefits associated with its mangrove initiatives, alongside additional economic value linked to fisheries productivity and ecosystem services. Independent research supports this direction: a 2020 Scientific Reports study estimates mangroves provide more than USD $65 billion annually in global flood protection benefits, with empirical evidence showing meaningful reductions in storm surge and coastal flood damage in protected regions.

Ecosystem Valuation as a Strategic Asset

While ecosystem valuation methods differ from traditional financial accounting, the broader significance is that ecosystem functions are increasingly expressed in economically structured terms relevant to institutional decision-making. For renewable infrastructure investors, these dynamics are increasingly relevant: solar, wind, transmission, and coastal assets depend on stable environmental conditions and resilient surrounding ecosystems. Degradation, erosion, biodiversity conflict, and flood exposure can introduce operational and insurance-related risks that directly affect project economics. As climate disclosure standards and nature-related risk frameworks mature, these factors are increasingly incorporated into diligence and valuation processes.

The Tokio Marine example reflects a broader market transition in which ecosystem services are increasingly evaluated in financially relevant terms. In parallel, a growing class of NbS and hybrid projects is beginning to generate measurable revenue streams alongside resilience benefits:

  • Agroforestry systems generate timber, food, and carbon revenues.
  • Blue carbon and forestry projects increasingly participate in voluntary carbon markets.
  • Agrivoltaics combine renewable energy generation with agricultural productivity on shared land, reducing land-use conflict that increasingly affects project siting and permitting timelines.
  • Bundled renewable-NbS developments, particularly in jurisdictions with biodiversity net gain requirements, are beginning to create hybrid revenue structures tied to both ecosystem and energy performance.

The Constraint Is Risk Profile, Not Absence of Returns

Early cash flows from NbS are often uneven, carbon revenues remain price-sensitive, and execution risk is higher due to technical and ecological complexity. This mirrors the early development of wind and solar, where concessional capital, guarantees, and development finance institutions were essential in bridging risk-return gaps before commercial scalability was achieved.

NbS and hybrid projects that generate both revenue and measurable resilience benefits are still frequently perceived as higher risk because these attributes are not yet consistently modeled in formats aligned with infrastructure investment standards. Institutional capital typically requires robust scenario analysis, sensitivity modeling, and transparent treatment of operational and environmental uncertainty — capabilities that NbS and conservation-linked projects are still developing.

The Need for Analytical Frameworks and Capital Structures

Closing this gap requires both improved analytical frameworks and capital structures that support early-stage project development. Blended finance structures can help bridge this transition by absorbing early-stage risk, funding technical validation, and supporting longer project maturation timelines that commercial capital alone may not initially tolerate. In the case of mangrove restoration, concessional or catalytic capital can help finance ecosystem rehabilitation upfront while insurers, governments, and infrastructure stakeholders benefit from reduced long-term exposure to flooding and coastal damage. Over time, improved data, standardized risk modeling, and demonstrated avoided-loss outcomes may allow these projects to transition toward more conventional infrastructure and resilience investment frameworks.

As climate-related disruptions intensify, the intersection of ecosystem resilience and infrastructure finance is becoming increasingly material for renewable energy markets. For dealmakers, the question is shifting from whether nature matters for asset value to how it can be consistently priced — a challenge that blended finance structures and avoided-loss frameworks are beginning to address.

Key Takeaways

  • Ecosystem resilience — flood exposure, biodiversity risk, erosion — is increasingly treated as a financial factor in renewable project diligence and valuation, not merely an environmental consideration.
  • NbS create value primarily through avoided losses, which is harder to capture in conventional project finance models than direct cash flow — this is the central challenge for scaling NbS investment.
  • The insurance sector (e.g., Tokio Marine’s mangrove program) provides an early, quantifiable signal of how ecosystem value translates into avoided claims and damage reduction.
  • Agrivoltaics, blue carbon markets, and bundled renewable-NbS developments are beginning to generate direct revenue alongside resilience benefits.
  • Blended finance — combining concessional, catalytic, and commercial capital — is the most promising near-term path to closing the NbS risk-return gap.
