Chapter 5: Environmental Due Diligence – Avoiding Hidden Liabilities

Why Environmental Due Diligence Matters

A wind developer acquires a 2,000-acre site in the Midwest. The turbines are permitted. The interconnection agreement is signed. The PPA is executed. The buyer is three weeks from closing.

An environmental consultant discovers that a prior industrial tenant operated a petroleum storage facility on a 40-acre portion of the parcel in the 1980s. Soil contamination extends 15 feet deep. Remediation is estimated at $8 million. The seller has no documentation of prior cleanup.

The deal does not close on schedule. The buyer must negotiate an environmental indemnity, escrow a remediation fund, and delay the project by 14 months.

This is not unusual. Environmental liabilities are among the most underestimated risks in renewable energy M&A.

This chapter explains the categories of environmental risk, how to identify them, and how to structure transactions to manage them effectively.

Categories of Environmental Risk

Phase I and Phase II Assessments

Environmental due diligence follows a standardized process:

Phase I Environmental Site Assessment (ESA): A review of historical records, aerial photographs, regulatory databases, and a site inspection to identify “Recognized Environmental Conditions” (RECs)—evidence of past contamination or hazardous materials use. Phase I does not involve soil or groundwater sampling.

Phase II ESA: If Phase I identifies RECs, a Phase II investigation involves soil and groundwater sampling to confirm or rule out contamination. Phase II results drive remediation decisions.

Phase III: Remediation design and implementation, if contamination is confirmed.

Common Environmental Liabilities

Environmental liabilities in renewable energy M&A arise from several sources:

Historical Industrial Use: Prior tenants may have operated gas stations, dry cleaners, manufacturing plants, or storage facilities—all potential contamination sources.

Agricultural Chemicals: Pesticides, herbicides, and fertilizers accumulate in soils on agricultural land. Certain legacy pesticides (DDT, chlordane) are persistent and require remediation.

Wetlands and Waters of the US: Federal and state law protects wetlands and certain water bodies. Construction on or near protected areas requires permits. Undisclosed wetland impacts can void permits and halt construction.

Threatened and Endangered Species: The presence of listed species or critical habitat triggers consultation requirements under the Endangered Species Act (ESA). Species surveys must be current and complete.

Cultural and Historic Resources: Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act requires consultation with tribal and historic preservation authorities. Undisclosed cultural resources can halt construction mid-project.

Stormwater and NPDES Permits: Construction activities require stormwater pollution prevention plans (SWPPPs) and National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) permits. Non-compliance is a liability.

Environmental Due Diligence Framework

Species and Habitat Risk

Biological surveys are among the most time-sensitive elements of environmental due diligence:

Migratory Birds: The Migratory Bird Treaty Act (MBTA) prohibits take of protected species. Wind turbines must be sited to minimize bird strike risk. Avian risk assessments require seasonal surveys that cannot be rushed.

Bats: Several bat species (Indiana bat, northern long-eared bat) are listed under the ESA. Acoustic surveys and habitat assessments are required, typically spanning multiple seasons.

Pollinators: Solar projects increasingly face scrutiny for impacts on pollinator habitat. Some utilities and states require pollinator-friendly vegetation plans.

Sage Grouse: In western states, greater sage-grouse habitat restrictions have blocked or significantly delayed wind and solar projects. Early assessment of sage-grouse risk is essential.

Wetland and Water Impacts

Wetland delineation is required for most large-scale renewable energy projects:

Delineation: A certified wetland scientist must walk the site and delineate all wetland boundaries per Army Corps of Engineers (ACOE) methodology.

Jurisdictional Determination: After delineation, the ACOE may issue a jurisdictional determination confirming which wetland areas fall under federal jurisdiction.

Permitting: Fill of jurisdictional wetlands requires a Section 404 permit. Mitigation (wetland creation or purchase of mitigation bank credits) is typically required.

Avoidance: The best practice is avoidance—design the project to avoid wetland areas entirely. This reduces permitting timelines and eliminates mitigation costs.

Case Study: The Wetland Surprise

A solar developer acquired a 600-acre site in the Southeast, relying on county land records that showed no wetland designations. The acquisition closed, permitting began, and an ACOE pre-construction survey identified 85 acres of jurisdictional wetlands—14% of the site—that bisected the planned array layout.

The developer had three options:

  1. Redesign the array to avoid all 85 wetland acres (reducing capacity by 18 MW)
  2. Fill 12 acres of wetlands under a Section 404 permit (requiring 36 acres of mitigation bank credits at $45,000 per credit)
  3. Abandon the project

The developer chose redesign. The project was delayed 11 months. Capital costs increased by $1.4 million due to redesign fees and interconnection re-study.

Lesson: Wetland delineation should occur in the early site control phase, before acquisition. Relying on county records or aerial photographs is insufficient.

Best Practices for Environmental Due Diligence

  1. Start Early: Commission Phase I ESA at letter of intent (LOI) stage, not after signing the purchase agreement.

  2. Use Qualified Consultants: Phase I ESAs should be conducted by licensed environmental professionals per ASTM E1527 standards. Do not use unqualified consultants to save money.

  3. Scope Phase II Broadly: If Phase I identifies any RECs, scope Phase II to include all potential source areas—not just the most obvious one.

  4. Budget for Contingency: Even clean sites carry a 5–10% contingency budget for unanticipated environmental findings post-acquisition.

  5. Negotiate Environmental Reps and Warranties: Purchase agreements should include seller representations about known environmental conditions, indemnities for pre-closing contamination, and escrow holdbacks for unresolved findings.

  6. Review Permits: Confirm all required environmental permits are in place, current, and transferable to the buyer.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental due diligence follows a phased process: Phase I (records/inspection), Phase II (sampling), Phase III (remediation).
  • Common liabilities include soil contamination, wetlands, endangered species, and cultural resources.
  • Environmental issues discovered post-closing are far more expensive than those discovered pre-closing.
  • Biological surveys are time-sensitive and cannot be compressed—plan for multi-season requirements.
  • Negotiate environmental indemnities, escrow holdbacks, and reps and warranties before closing.
