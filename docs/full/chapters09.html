Accurately Assessing Worth and Timing in Renewable Energy M&A

Accurate valuation is a fundamental component of successful M&A transactions. The Commercial Operation Date (COD) is critical in evaluating renewable project acquisitions because it signifies the point at which the project is fully operational, grid-connected, and ready to produce energy under its Power Purchase Agreement (PPA). This date triggers major financial events, such as the release of final project funding, the start of the off-taker’s obligation to purchase power at the contracted rate, and the transition from construction to operational security. An earlier COD can affect valuation by accelerating revenue generation and decreasing project risk, potentially making the asset more appealing to acquirers.

Why Commercial Operation Date is Important for Acquisitions

Revenue Generation Trigger

The COD marks the beginning of revenue-generating activities, as the off-taker is contractually obligated to purchase power from the project under the PPA.

Financing Milestones

Achieving COD often aligns with the release of remaining project funding and tax equity investments, essential for the project’s completion and profitability. Delays in survey completion, unresolved title matters, or failure to satisfy ALTA endorsement requirements can directly impact financing timelines, construction loan closings, and tax equity transactions. Developers should coordinate survey, title, underwriting, and financing teams early to avoid last-minute closing disruptions.

Tax Equity Closings and Title Readiness

In renewable energy tax-equity transactions, site control is one of the most critical components of financing certainty. Tax equity investors, lenders, and underwriting teams require clear evidence that lease portfolios, easements, rights-of-way, and title documentation fully support long-term project operations and tax credit qualification timelines. Even minor title defects or unresolved survey issues can delay funding or jeopardize ITC/PTC qualification. A three-step framework follows:

Site-Control Audit

  • Verify leases
  • Easements
  • ROWs
  • Legal descriptions
  • Subordinations
  • Encroachments

Title-Risk Reserve

  • Escrow known defects
  • Allocate residual exposure
  • Endorsement strategy

Unified Closing Protocol

  • Coordinated checklist
  • Synchronized signatures
  • Survey sign-offs
  • Document control

Title-Risk Reserve

Developers and tax-equity investors frequently utilize title-risk reserves or targeted escrows to manage unresolved defects while preserving financing schedules. Structured release mechanisms tied to curative milestones can help align incentives between sponsors, investors, title underwriters, and lenders while limiting open-ended exposure.

Project De-risking

Projects sold before COD are essentially “developer fee” transactions, where value is derived from completing development milestones. Projects at or near COD are significantly de-risked, making them more valuable to investors.

Residual Risk Management

In some transactions, residual title exposure may be managed through structured escrows, endorsements, or negotiated underwriting solutions rather than complete pre-closing curative resolution. These tools can help preserve financing schedules while limiting open-ended exposure for lenders, investors, and sponsors.

“Project Slippage” as a Valuation Risk

Buyers and investors increasingly evaluate “project slippage” risk when assessing renewable energy portfolios. Delays involving permitting, title curative, interconnection, mineral rights, or community opposition may materially reduce the likelihood that projects will meet financing and construction deadlines, affecting valuation and marketability.

Minor Defects Can Create Major Financing Problems

Minor title defects, survey inconsistencies, or lease discrepancies discovered late in the financing process can create disproportionate impacts on renewable energy transactions, including lender holdbacks, delayed funding, increased curative costs, and threats to ITC/PTC timelines.

Performance Guarantees

COD sets the baseline for post-operation performance guarantees, including energy output, which can trigger penalties if not met.

PPA Security Transition

The PPA’s financial security requirements shift from construction-period security to post-commercial operation security at COD, impacting the overall financial profile of the asset and valuation.

Accelerated Cash Flows

A project closer to or at COD can be valued higher because it will generate revenue and cash flow sooner, compared to a pre-COD project requiring development.

Reduced Risk

Projects close to COD are considered less risky since construction and interconnection are done and the project is generating power.

Valuation Before and After COD

One significant factor for assessing potential project success and valuation involves having a clear understanding of where value lies within project lifecycles. For acquisitions before COD, the price reflects the value of the development work and the potential for future revenue. For acquisitions after COD, the price is based on the operational asset’s proven cash flow and ongoing performance.

A thorough understanding of due diligence findings and project timing is required to establish a project’s worth. At the beginning of many M&A projects, we were informed that the project is shovel ready. That can mean many different things to different people. Therefore, a clear understanding of every facet of the project is essential.

Lease Expiration vs COD/PTC Timing

Developers should evaluate lease expiration schedules against anticipated financing milestones, COD targets, and tax credit qualification deadlines. A lease lapse prior to financing or commercial operation can create material transaction and valuation risk.

Overlay Lease Calendars Against COD/PTC Timelines

Developers should evaluate lease expiration schedules against anticipated COD, ITC, and PTC qualification deadlines. Lease lapses or unresolved extensions prior to financing or in-service milestones can materially affect project valuation, tax-credit eligibility, and transaction certainty.

The Impact of Timing on Valuation

  • Commercial Operation Date (COD) Targets: The COD is pivotal in determining a project’s value, especially when aligned with market demand or regulatory incentives.
  • Market Conditions: Energy prices, technology costs, and policy environments can fluctuate, affecting project economics.

Developers should periodically conduct strategic pipeline reviews to identify which projects are most likely to achieve COD within required financing or tax-credit timelines. Projects facing significant title, permitting, interconnection, community, or site-control risks may require restructuring, acceleration, or monetization strategies before market conditions change.

Policy Changes Compress M&A Timelines

Legislative and policy changes can suddenly compress the entire transaction market. Legislative changes, tax credit revisions, and evolving “start construction” requirements can rapidly compress renewable energy development and M&A timelines. Developers may face increased pressure to advance projects into construction, accelerate financing activities, or monetize assets quickly before incentive qualification windows narrow.

Adjusting Valuation Based on Due Diligence

  • Risk Assessment: Quantify risks identified during due diligence such as legal issues, and environmental concerns. Adjust the discount rate or required return to reflect these risks.
  • Underwriting Deficiencies: Unclear policy language creates litigation risk.
  • Cost Implications: Estimate additional costs required to resolve issues (such as legal fees, remediation efforts). Incorporate these costs into the financial model.
  • Timing Delays: Reassess cash flow projections based on potential delays. Consider the impact on incentives tied to specific deadlines such as tax credits.

Permitting Risk Should Affect Valuation

Community opposition and permitting uncertainty should be incorporated into project risk assessments and valuation models. Developers and acquirers should evaluate public sentiment, local political dynamics, prior permitting history, and evidence of organized opposition when assessing project timelines and financial exposure.

Valuation Methodologies

  • Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): Projects future cash flows and discounts them to present value.
  • Comparable Transactions: Analyzes similar deals to gauge market value.
  • Current Market Conditions: Analyze tax incentives, interest rates and related market conditions.
  • Replacement Cost: Considers the cost to replicate the project from scratch.

Negotiation Strategies

  • Price Adjustments: Negotiate purchase price reductions to account for identified issues.
  • Earn-Outs: Structure deals where part of the payment is contingent on achieving certain milestones.
  • Indemnities and Warranties: Include contractual protections against specific risks.

Cost & Risk

At the outset, a comprehensive audit of all documents will reveal potential risk factors. Every issue can be boiled down to a cost. Once a cost is assigned to it, then a strategy for mitigation can be developed. Will you use insurance or another instrument to provide indemnity for these actions? At this juncture, an understanding of the cost and exposure to risk is critical.

Residual Risk Management

Not all title defects require immediate physical resolution prior to closing. In some cases, developers and lenders may utilize escrows, endorsements, indemnities, or structured underwriting solutions to manage residual risk while preserving financing timelines.

Tribal land transactions may involve additional financing and enforceability considerations because sovereign immunity, federal trust structures, and jurisdictional limitations can affect lender remedies, collateral rights, and dispute resolution procedures. Developers and investors should engage experienced legal counsel and title professionals early when evaluating projects involving tribal lands.

Case Study Insight

A company acquired a project expecting a COD in 2026. Due diligence revealed permitting delays that could push COD to 2028, significantly affecting the project’s net present value. The company renegotiated the purchase price to reflect the new timeline and associated risks.

Conclusion

Accurate valuation requires integrating due diligence findings and realistic timelines into financial assessments, ensuring that the investment aligns with the company’s strategic and financial objectives. Failed or poorly managed projects can create reputational impacts beyond a single developer or transaction. Communities often view renewable energy projects collectively rather than distinguishing among individual companies, making effective stakeholder engagement and responsible development practices increasingly important across the industry.
