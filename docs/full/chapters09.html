Accurately Assessing Worth and Timing in Renewable Energy M&A

Accurate valuation is a fundamental component of successful M&A transactions. The Commercial Operation Date (COD) is critical in evaluating renewable project acquisitions because it signifies the point at which the project is fully operational, grid-connected, and ready to produce energy under its Power Purchase Agreement (PPA). This date triggers major financial events, such as the release of final project funding, the start of the off-taker’s obligation to purchase power at the contracted rate, and the transition from construction to operational security. An earlier COD can affect valuation by accelerating revenue generation and decreasing project risk, potentially making the asset more appealing to acquirers.

Why Commercial Operation Date is Important for Acquisitions

Revenue Generation Trigger

The COD marks the beginning of revenue-generating activities, as the off-taker is contractually obligated to purchase power from the project under the PPA.

Financing Milestones

Achieving COD often aligns with the release of remaining project funding and tax equity investments, essential for the project’s completion and profitability. Delays in survey completion, unresolved title matters, or failure to satisfy ALTA endorsement requirements can directly impact financing timelines, construction loan closings, and tax equity transactions. Developers should coordinate survey, title, underwriting, and financing teams early to avoid last-minute closing disruptions.

Tax Equity Closings and Title Readiness

In renewable energy tax-equity transactions, site control is one of the most critical components of financing certainty. Tax equity investors, lenders, and underwriting teams require clear evidence that lease portfolios, easements, rights-of-way, and title documentation fully support long-term project operations and tax credit qualification timelines. Even minor title defects or unresolved survey issues can delay funding or jeopardize ITC/PTC qualification. A three-step framework follows:

Site-Control Audit

  • Verify leases
  • Easements
  • ROWs
  • Legal descriptions
  • Subordinations
  • Encroachments

Title-Risk Reserve

  • Escrow known defects
  • Allocate residual exposure
  • Endorsement strategy

Unified Closing Protocol

  • Coordinated checklist
  • Synchronized signatures
  • Survey sign-offs
  • Document control

Title-Risk Reserve

Developers and tax-equity investors frequently utilize title-risk reserves or targeted escrows to manage unresolved defects while preserving financing schedules. Structured release mechanisms tied to curative milestones can help align incentives between sponsors, investors, title underwriters, and lenders while limiting open-ended exposure.

Project De-risking

Projects sold before COD are essentially “developer fee” transactions, where value is derived from completing development milestones. Projects at or near COD are significantly de-risked, making them more valuable to investors.

Residual Risk Management

In some transactions, residual title exposure may be managed through structured escrows, endorsements, or negotiated underwriting solutions rather than complete pre-closing curative resolution. These tools can help preserve financing schedules while limiting open-ended exposure for lenders, investors, and sponsors.

“Project Slippage” as a Valuation Risk

Buyers and investors increasingly evaluate “project slippage” risk when assessing renewable energy portfolios. Delays involving permitting, title curative, interconnection, mineral rights, or community opposition may materially reduce the likelihood that projects will meet financing and construction deadlines, affecting valuation and marketability.

Minor Defects Can Create Major Financing Problems

Minor title defects, survey inconsistencies, or lease discrepancies discovered late in the financing process can create disproportionate impacts on renewable energy transactions, including lender holdbacks, delayed funding, increased curative costs, and threats to ITC/PTC timelines.

Performance Guarantees

COD sets the baseline for post-operation performance guarantees, including energy output, which can trigger penalties if not met.

PPA Security Transition

The PPA’s financial security requirements shift from construction-period security to post-commercial operation security at COD, impacting the overall financial profile of the asset and valuation.

Accelerated Cash Flows

A project closer to or at COD can be valued higher because it will generate revenue and cash flow sooner, compared to a pre-COD project requiring development.

Reduced Risk

Projects close to COD are considered less risky since construction and interconnection are done and the project is generating power.

Valuation Before and After COD

One significant factor for assessing potential project success and valuation involves having a clear understanding of where value lies within project lifecycles. For acquisitions before COD, the price reflects the value of the development work and the potential for future revenue. For acquisitions after COD, the price is based on the operational asset’s proven cash flow and ongoing performance.

A thorough understanding of due diligence findings and project timing is required to establish a project’s worth. At the beginning of many M&A projects, we were informed that the project is shovel ready. That can mean many different things to different people. Therefore, a clear understanding of every facet of the project is essential.

Lease Expiration vs COD/PTC Timing

Developers should evaluate lease expiration schedules against anticipated financing milestones, COD targets, and tax credit qualification deadlines. A lease lapse prior to financing or commercial operation can create material transaction and valuation risk.

Overlay Lease Calendars Against COD/PTC Timelines

Developers should evaluate lease expiration schedules against anticipated COD, ITC, and PTC qualification deadlines. Lease lapses or unresolved extensions prior to financing or in-service milestones can materially affect project valuation, tax-credit eligibility, and transaction certainty.

The Impact of Timing on Valuation

  • Commercial Operation Date (COD) Targets: The COD is pivotal in determining a project’s value, especially when aligned with market demand or regulatory incentives.
  • Market Conditions: Energy prices, technology costs, and policy environments can fluctuate, affecting project economics.

Developers should periodically conduct strategic pipeline reviews to identify which projects are most likely to achieve COD within required financing or tax-credit timelines. Projects facing significant title, permitting, interconnection, community, or site-control risks may require restructuring, acceleration, or monetization strategies before market conditions change.

Policy Changes Compress M&A Timelines

Legislative and policy changes can suddenly compress the entire transaction market. Legislative changes, tax credit revisions, and evolving “start construction” requirements can rapidly compress renewable energy development and M&A timelines. Developers may face increased pressure to advance projects into construction, accelerate financing activities, or monetize assets quickly before incentive qualification windows narrow.

Adjusting Valuation Based on Due Diligence

  • Risk Assessment: Quantify risks identified during due diligence such as legal issues, and environmental concerns. Adjust the discount rate or required return to reflect these risks.
  • Underwriting Deficiencies: Unclear policy language creates litigation risk.
  • Cost Implications: Estimate additional costs required to resolve issues (such as legal fees, remediation efforts). Incorporate these costs into the financial model.
  • Timing Delays: Reassess cash flow projections based on potential delays. Consider the impact on incentives tied to specific deadlines such as tax credits.

Permitting Risk Should Affect Valuation

Community opposition and permitting uncertainty should be incorporated into project risk assessments and valuation models. Developers and acquirers should evaluate public sentiment, local political dynamics, prior permitting history, and evidence of organized opposition when assessing project timelines and financial exposure.

Valuation Methodologies

  • Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): Projects future cash flows and discounts them to present value.
  • Comparable Transactions: Analyzes similar deals to gauge market value.
  • Current Market Conditions: Analyze tax incentives, interest rates and related market conditions.
  • Replacement Cost: Considers the cost to replicate the project from scratch.

Negotiation Strategies

  • Price Adjustments: Negotiate purchase price reductions to account for identified issues.
  • Earn-Outs: Structure deals where part of the payment is contingent on achieving certain milestones.
  • Indemnities and Warranties: Include contractual protections against specific risks.

Cost & Risk

At the outset, a comprehensive audit of all documents will reveal potential risk factors. Every issue can be boiled down to a cost. Once a cost is assigned to it, then a strategy for mitigation can be developed. Will you use insurance or another instrument to provide indemnity for these actions? At this juncture, an understanding of the cost and exposure to risk is critical.

In the years ahead, it will be essential to effectively distribute risk among stakeholders and strategically utilize relationships to meet stringent deadlines. It is important to ensure that these relationships and expertise are in place, as they will be essential for understanding the situation we are facing.

Title insurance for projects is one of the ways to identify and mitigate risk. We recently closed a rare case where we had to file a title claim on behalf of our client. We were successful in obtaining coverage for the legal costs involved in resolving the issue. We recently concluded a rare title claim case for our client. The title company ended up covering the legal resolution costs.

Residual Risk Management

Not all title defects require immediate physical resolution prior to closing. In some cases, developers and lenders may utilize escrows, endorsements, indemnities, or structured underwriting solutions to manage residual risk while preserving financing timelines.

Tribal land transactions may involve additional financing and enforceability considerations because sovereign immunity, federal trust structures, and jurisdictional limitations can affect lender remedies, collateral rights, and dispute resolution procedures. Developers and investors should engage experienced legal counsel and title professionals early when evaluating projects involving tribal lands.

Case Study Insight

A company acquired a project expecting a COD in 2026. Due diligence revealed permitting delays that could push COD to 2028, significantly affecting the project’s net present value. The company renegotiated the purchase price to reflect the new timeline and associated risks.

Conclusion

Accurate valuation requires integrating due diligence findings and realistic timelines into financial assessments, ensuring that the investment aligns with the company’s strategic and financial objectives. Failed or poorly managed projects can create reputational impacts beyond a single developer or transaction. Communities often view renewable energy projects collectively rather than distinguishing among individual companies, making effective stakeholder engagement and responsible development practices increasingly important across the industry.

Is the land you’re getting, really your land? In the realm of renewable energy projects, land is a fundamental asset. Before finalizing any acquisition, it’s imperative to verify land control and access to ensure that the project rests on solid ground — literally and legally.

Land control is the cornerstone of every successful renewable energy project. It assures that a developer has the legal authority to enter, use, and develop a specified plot of land. Without appropriate site control, even the most promising projects might suffer major delays, legal battles, or catastrophic failure.

Critical Path Parcels

Developers should identify critical path parcels early in diligence — substations, transmission corridors, access routes, collector systems, and key interconnection areas. Title defects or site-control failures affecting these parcels can create disproportionate impacts on financing, construction sequencing, and COD schedules.

Renewable energy projects typically acquire property via leases, easements, or purchase agreements. Developers should work closely with title companies and ALTA surveyors to identify required Table A items and endorsement requirements early — optional survey certifications frequently become mandatory lender or title requirements during financing and closing.

Government Land Complexity and Overlapping Rights

Developers often assume that acquiring rights from a state or local government agency will be more straightforward than negotiating with private landowners. In practice, government-owned land introduces an entirely different layer of complexity.

State trust lands, public university lands, water districts, county-owned property, and other public holdings frequently involve overlapping interests not immediately visible during preliminary diligence. A developer may secure a lease only to discover that another party retains grazing rights, farming rights, mineral interests, water access rights, or operational easements affecting the same property — and in some cases, these third-party rights can directly interfere with substations, access corridors, or interconnection infrastructure.

Checkerboard Ownership

One of the most common mistakes developers make is assuming that site control over one parcel automatically guarantees access across adjoining parcels. In many Western states, renewable energy projects cross “checkerboard” ownership patterns involving combinations of state, federal, tribal, and private land. Each parcel may carry different approval standards, title requirements, timelines, and regulatory obligations. Legal access across one tract does not necessarily establish access rights across the next.

Public vs. Private Sector Timelines

Public-sector transactions frequently operate on timelines different from private transactions. Many state and local agencies face staffing shortages, outdated systems, and increasing development volume. Even after commercial terms are negotiated, agreements may require additional review by agency counsel, governing boards, or state attorneys general before execution — minor revisions to legal descriptions, formatting, or access provisions can delay approvals for weeks or months.

Successful development on government land requires proactive communication, relationship management, and coordination across stakeholders. Experienced developers treat public-sector entities as long-term project partners rather than transactional counterparties — verifying all existing rights affecting the property, confirming legal access across adjoining tracts, prioritizing critical infrastructure parcels early, and building additional time into schedules for administrative review.

Why Land Control Matters Across the Project Lifecycle

Feasibility Studies: Land control is critical during first feasibility studies — environmental assessments and compliance review cannot proceed without site control.

Securing Financing: Lenders and investors require developers to demonstrate land control before financing can be obtained. Without it, a project is considered high-risk.

Permits and Regulatory Compliance: Zoning boards and environmental authorities may refuse to process permits unless the developer demonstrates ownership or lease rights.

Managing Risk: Acquiring land control early allows developers to proactively address ownership conflicts, title issues, or community objections before they derail the project.

Protecting Project Timelines: Renewable projects are time-sensitive, particularly with incentive schemes or financing deadlines tied to specific dates.

Failure to confirm land control can lead to legal disputes, operational delays, and financial losses from litigation, lease renegotiation, or cancellation.

Case Study: The Access Road in Fallbrook

We once worked on a storage project in Fallbrook, California. The property sat away from the main road and was not visible from the frontage road. We believed all necessary rights had been secured — until inspectors at the fire department raised concerns about the site’s distance from the main road. After inspection, the fire department required a second access point at the back of the project, requiring new agreements with landowners that the developer had not anticipated. The added expense covered design and engineering of a second access route. Even a route that looks drivable from point A to point B may not be insurable due to fire-code or other regulatory requirements that only surface during inspection.

Land Control & Ownership Disputes

Landowner disputes account for roughly 40% of potential issues encountered in land verification. Title ownership can be held in trust, through corporations, or passed down through inheritance — an heir who says “I control everything” may not actually be on title. Understanding who has true authority over the property is critical before any agreement is signed.

Case Study: Clearing a Legacy Road Reservation to Ensure Smooth Closing Submitted by Senior Title Insurance Expert Tara Brown-Selders

A developer was advancing a battery storage project in Virginia when title review surfaced a historical municipal roadway reservation. Although the road had never been built and the area had not developed as originally envisioned, the reservation remained in the title record — and the proposed alignment would have cut directly through the planned storage site.

Risk: Even though never built, the municipality technically retained the right to construct the road. The title insurer needed confirmation the municipality no longer intended to pursue it before coverage could be finalized.

Strategy: Rather than waiting for a compressed closing timeline, the developer, legal counsel, title team, and closing team worked together early. A knowledgeable title insurance sales representative — who understood both the developer’s priorities and underwriting requirements — kept the issue visible and helped clarify exactly what language the municipality would need to provide.

Outcome: The municipality provided a confirmation letter stating it had no intent to construct the roadway. Because the issue was flagged months in advance, the letter was secured several weeks before closing, and the transaction proceeded without delay.

Key Takeaway: Early diligence and proactive communication can prevent historical land issues from becoming closing obstacles — and an experienced title professional who understands both the development timeline and underwriting requirements can keep complex issues moving toward resolution.

Power of Attorney

Power of attorney is often statutory in nature, requiring strict adherence to compliance regulations and specific signing protocols. Risks related to unconfirmed authority can produce invalid or unenforceable leases, delays, added costs, ownership disputes, and litigation. Provide the title company with the power of attorney, trust certificate, or corporate records needed to verify signing authority. Small details matter — a different middle initial (George E. Smith vs. George R. Smith) may indicate two different people, and suffixes like Jr. and Sr. must be confirmed to ensure the right parties are under contract.

Joint Tenancy, Trusts & Unrecorded Rights

Developers can be caught off guard by joint tenancy and trusts, and unrecorded rights can be a significant problem even when a title search appears thorough. Landowners often know things the record doesn’t show: capped wells, historical mineral production, an adjacent owner’s access arrangement, or conservation reserve easements that affect how and where you can develop. An apparent access route may not constitute an insurable right — title insurers will require that you “perfect” it, which may mean creating curative documents.

Select a title company with expertise in ALTA coverage and renewable energy transactions, and one that can support the transaction size — not all title carriers can write coverage at the scale many renewable deals require. Establish those relationships early and be clear about your project requirements.

Farmlands & Unrecorded Rights

Grazing and farming leases and water rights are often off-record but represent real third-party interests that need to be addressed. Some jurisdictions, including Illinois, specify deadlines by which notice must be given to farming tenants — miss the notification date and a construction start date tied to it may need to shift.

The Wetlands Challenge

Developing on or near wetlands requires balancing energy needs against the imperative to preserve biodiversity, water quality, and flood control functions. Construction can disturb wetland habitats and the plant and animal life they support, and projects are often subject to regulations such as the North American Wetlands Conservation Act. Ground-mounted systems may require specialized piling techniques to minimize disturbance. Wetlands should be considered during site selection — proximity to water can sometimes shorten cabling runs, but the regulatory complexity often outweighs that benefit.

Key Steps in Verifying Land Control

  • Cross-reference leases and maps; ensure the geographical area in the map aligns with legal descriptions in lease agreements.
  • Use GIS to accurately map land parcels.
  • Review vesting deeds establishing current ownership and identify discrepancies between deedholders and lease signatories.
  • Check title reports for liens, encumbrances, or easements — recognizing that early-stage projects may not have had full title work performed.

ALTA Surveys and Advanced Title Review: Protecting Site Control

Site control is far more than a signed lease or a marked parcel on a map. Sophisticated buyers, lenders, tax equity investors, and title insurers increasingly expect developers to demonstrate that the legal description aligns precisely with physical realities on the ground.

Too often, early-stage projects rely on outdated boundary sketches or limited title work. A fence crossing a property line, an undefined access route, an abandoned irrigation ditch, or an unrecorded utility easement may seem minor, yet each can materially affect layout, financing certainty, insurability, and the ability to close.

An ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey identifies visible encroachments, access routes, rights-of-way, improvements, utility corridors, and easements affecting the property, and establishes accurate monumentation. Interconnection corridors, collector lines, substations, battery storage locations, turbine setbacks, and access roads all rely on accurate legal descriptions — inconsistencies discovered after financing documents are drafted can trigger expensive curative work, redesign costs, delayed closings, or threats to tax-credit qualification timelines.

Not all ALTA surveys provide the same protection. Many lenders and institutional investors now require full Tier-1 ALTA surveys with extended coverage and lender-specific certifications. Limited survey products that reduce upfront cost frequently shift risk downstream into financing and closing, where remediation is more expensive and time-sensitive.

A survey alone cannot identify all project risks — advanced title review must examine historical conveyances, easements, restrictions, mortgages, rights-of-way, and prior reservations. Some of the most significant risks (neighbor access arrangements, oral farming leases, historical roadway reservations, irrigation rights, unrecorded utility agreements, conservation restrictions, legacy covenants) are not immediately visible in public records. Experienced teams work with title counsel, surveyors, and underwriters to clarify exceptions and obtain accurate exhibits — through boundary line agreements, easement relocations, subordination agreements, corrective deeds, or escrow arrangements.

Challenges in Land Verification

  • Incomplete Documentation: Early-stage projects might not have comprehensive land records.
  • Time Constraints: Tight M&A timelines may limit the depth of verification possible.
  • Complex Ownership Structures: Land may be owned by multiple parties or entities, complicating verification.

Best Practices

  • Engage Land Specialists: Professionals with land acquisition and title experience identify issues that might be overlooked.
  • Utilize Public Records: County records, property tax records, and other public databases supplement provided documentation.
  • Prioritize Critical Parcels: Focus on substation sites, main access points, and other infrastructure-critical land.

Key Takeaways

  • Land control is the legal foundation that enables financing, permitting, and construction — without it, nothing else proceeds.
  • Government-owned and checkerboard parcels introduce timeline and approval risks that differ fundamentally from private transactions.
  • Roughly 40% of land issues trace back to ownership disputes; small details (middle initials, suffixes, trust language) matter.
  • A Tier-1 ALTA survey combined with advanced title review is the strongest protection against late-discovered defects.
  • Early, proactive communication — especially with municipalities and title professionals — converts potential closing obstacles into manageable curative work.
