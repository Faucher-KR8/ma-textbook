Chapter Three

Ensuring Correct Ownership – Getting the Signatures Right

Before a renewable energy project can be built, developers must secure legal rights to use the land. This includes leases, purchase options, and access agreements. However, delays or gaps in site control can stall the project and alarm investors. Securing valid agreements hinges on obtaining signatures from all rightful owners of the land. Overlooking ownership complexities can render leases unenforceable and introduce significant risks. Ownership verification is critical. Have the right people signed? Have you reviewed deeds? Has title search been completed?

During early development or when reviewing a project for a merger or acquisition, it is important to be aware of five common mistakes:

1. Assuming Legal Access Exists

A road shown on a map does not guarantee legal access. Easements may be missing, expired, or simply not recorded. Check to see whether there is a deeded easement. If the project relies on verbal agreements, historical arrangements, or goodwill, it is at risk. Prioritize key areas essential for project infrastructure or main access points. Confirm legal access for all parcels; identify and record necessary easements.

2. Not Verifying Ownership Across the Entire Site

Multiple individuals can share ownership. This is particularly common with large or inherited family lands. Even worse, records may not reflect recent changes like deaths, trusts, or quitclaims. If an owner is missing from a signature block, the lease or option might not be valid. Initiate curative work to identify and resolve ownership discrepancies, outdated instruments, and missing interests. Engaging land specialists can expedite this process.

3. Skipping the Title Commitment Review

The title commitment is an important document. This is where red flags hide: mineral rights carve-outs, old mortgages, utility easements, or overlapping rights from past deals. These are frequently overlooked deal breakers. Order and review title commitments, identifying exceptions related to mineral leases, water rights, environmental laws, or cultural heritage sites.

4. Letting Land Agreements Sit Without Updates

Did you sign a letter of intent or option years ago? Expired, incorrect, or missing land parcels need renegotiation. Your agreement is only as strong as its paper trail and expiration clause. Align ALTA survey boundaries with title and landowner agreements. Cross reference leases and maps with legal descriptions in lease agreements. Ensure that all site control documents are valid, assignable, and fully executed.

5. Assuming Curative Work Can Wait

Curative work takes time. If you wait to fix gaps in ownership, access, or title, it’s often too late. Probate, corporate consents, and lien releases can take months. We’ve witnessed renewable energy projects stopped in their tracks due to unforeseen legal challenges related to the land. Title Curative is an essential part of the process when it comes to large-scale renewable energy projects. It includes securing necessary consents, subordinations, and crossing permits to achieve successful project financing. Research deed documents and conduct boundary surveys to obtain accurate title and legal description information, ensuring you can proceed confidently. Start early, everything takes longer than you think.

Curative Is a Relay Race

Title curative is rarely a one-time exercise. It requires ongoing coordination among land teams, surveyors, legal counsel, title underwriters, lenders, and developers. Successful projects establish clear communication channels, assign ownership responsibility early, and maintain structured follow-up processes to ensure defects are resolved before financing deadlines.

Title Curative Is the Linchpin of Financing

Title curative is a transaction-management discipline. Financing timelines drive curative urgency. Title curative is often the linchpin of a successful financing close. Systematically identifying defects, prioritizing cures, coordinating stakeholders, and managing residual exposure through escrows and endorsements can materially reduce transaction delays and improve financing certainty.

Common Ownership Complexities

  • Life Estates: A life estate grants an individual rights to a property for their lifetime. Example: A property deeded to Mr. and Mrs. Smith with a reservation that Grandma retains a life estate. Implication: If Grandma hasn’t signed the lease, the agreement is incomplete and not insurable.
  • Joint Tenancy and Tenancy in Common: Properties owned by multiple individuals, each with equal or proportionate shares. All owners must consent and sign agreements for them to be valid.
  • Trusts and Corporate Entities: Properties held in trusts or by corporations require signatures from authorized representatives. Verification of authority is essential.
  • Unrecorded Interests: Rights or claims not reflected in public records, such as verbal agreements or unrecorded easements.

Strategies for Ensuring Correct Ownership and Signatories

  • Conduct Thorough Title Searches: Identify all current owners and their respective interests. Review for any reservations, easements, liens, or other encumbrances.
  • Review Vesting Deeds in Detail: Check vesting deeds and title history for irregularities. Pay attention to reservations like life estates or mineral rights.
  • Verify Authority of Signatories: Ensure that individuals signing on behalf of entities have the legal authority to do so. Obtain corporate resolutions or trustee certifications when necessary.
  • Consult Legal Experts: Engage attorneys specializing in real estate and energy transactions. Address complex scenarios proactively.
  • Potential Risks of Neglecting Ownership Verification: Invalid leases, agreements may be deemed unenforceable, affecting project legality.
  • Delays and Added Costs: Time and money spent resolving ownership disputes.
  • Insurability Issues: Title insurance may not cover leases with incomplete ownership consent.
Warning

Case Study – Life Estates

In one instance, a developer failed to recognize that a life estate holder had not signed the lease. When the oversight was discovered during the financing phase, the project faced significant delays as the developer had to renegotiate with the life estate holder, who then demanded more favorable terms.

During a recent closing, it was necessary to obtain signed lease amendments from all landowners involved. Learning that one woman’s health was failing, we prioritized her signing of the amendment so it could be recorded quickly. She passed away the day after signing, just as we were recording the amendment. She had 13 heirs who would have had to sign any subsequent documents. We were grateful to our community for helping us gain this insight and react quickly. Waiting would have postponed the closing for weeks due to probate and identifying and locating the heirs.

We have an issue right now with a project where the mother is incapacitated and her daughter will potentially sign in two capacities — as her successor under a trust, and under a power of attorney, each document having their own unique issues. To have the daughter sign for the trust we need the physician’s letter in compliance with HIPAA and the terms of the trust, confirming the mother’s incapacity. In such cases, the power of attorney must be durable and authorize real estate transactions.

Conclusion

Ensuring that all rightful owners have signed agreements is not just a legal necessity but a cornerstone of project security and bankability.
