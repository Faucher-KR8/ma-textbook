Hidden Layers - Addressing Complex Ownership Structures in Project Acquisitions

M&A has become one of the fastest ways to fill pipeline gaps in the renewable sector. With interconnection queues stretching for years and greenfield development slowing under regulatory and community pressure, acquiring projects can be the most effective path forward. But there’s a catch. Deals that look strong on paper can fall apart during closing. The reason? Risks are often buried in the details, such as land ownership issues or missing signatures that weren’t uncovered early enough.

Every deal carries risk. The question is whether you identify those risks early, when they can be managed, or later, when they threaten valuation and delay closing of the deal.

Ownership structures can be intricate, with various interests that may not be immediately apparent. Companies often form partnerships to develop projects. This requires a clear understanding of the agreements, Identifying and resolving these complexities is essential to securing valid agreements.

Hidden Ownership Complexities

Ownership isn’t always straightforward. Overlooking a single signatory can render a lease unenforceable. Unrecorded rights, which often exist, can completely derail financing. Common ownership issues include:

  • Life Estates: If the life tenant hasn’t signed, the agreements are incomplete.
  • Trusts & Estates: Require specific authorization to bind the entity.
  • Easements and rights-of-way allow third parties to use parts of the property, potentially limiting development or requiring adjustments.
  • Undivided Interests: All co-owners must consent to lease terms.
  • Encumbrances and Liens: Claims by creditors or other parties against the property, that need to be resolved or subordinated.
  • Probate Complications: Missing heirs can stall transactions for years.
  • Off-record Agreements: Grazing leases, water rights, or off-record access can create conflicts.

It is critical to look beyond the deed. Confirm signatory authority, identify any unrecorded interests through landowner conversations. Don’t assume that what is disclosed in the title report tells the whole story. Let’s examine frequently encountered complexities in greater detail.

Life Estates

A life estate is a legal ownership interest in real property that lasts for the duration of a person’s life, known as the “life tenant.” This arrangement divides ownership into two interests: the right to possess and use the property during the tenant’s lifetime and the right to receive the property after their death, called the “remainderman.” The life tenant cannot leave the property to their heirs, as their interest automatically ends upon their death.

The remainderman is the individual or party who receives full ownership of the property after the life tenant’s death. For example, a deed might read, “To John Doe for life, then to Jane Doe.” In this case, John receives a life estate and can use the property during his lifetime. Upon John’s death, Jane Doe automatically receives full ownership of the property.

The duration of the life estate is measured by the life of the life tenant, or another specified person (known as a life estate pur autre vie). The individual holding lifetime rights, which is the life tenant, must provide his or her consent to leases or agreements affecting their interest. Therefore, it is imperative that the life tenant’s signature is on all agreements related to their property.

Trusts & Estates

A trust is a legal arrangement established to manage and distribute assets. Ownership of those assets moves from the original owner to the trust. The person or organization managing the trust takes on a fiduciary role, protecting the assets to the benefit of the beneficiaries. The trust sets conditions that must be met for the assets to eventually transfer from the trust to its beneficiaries.

There are three types of stakeholders involved in a trust:

  • The grantor. This is the person who places their assets into the trust.
  • The trustee. The trustee oversees the assets in the trust. They enter into the agreement knowing that they must manage those assets in the best interests of the beneficiaries.
  • The beneficiary or beneficiaries. Assuming the conditions of the trust are met, the beneficiaries receive the assets held in that trust.

There are several types of trusts, categories include revocable trusts (can be changed by the grantor while alive) and irrevocable trusts (difficult or impossible to change once established). Establishing a trust allows the grantor and their beneficiaries to avoid probate court. Probate can cause major delays, because all heirs must sign documents related to transactions. Assets are held in a trust are no longer part of an individual’s estate. Trust property requires trustee action and verification of both their authority and the terms of trust.

Estate generally refers to the total assets and liabilities (debts) a person or legal entity owns at a particular point in time, or upon death. However, the specific legal definition can vary depending on the context and scope of the estate.

Easements and Rights-of-Way

An easement is a legal right for a non-owner to use another person’s land for a specific purpose, while a right-of-way is a specific type of easement that grants the right to travel or pass across another person’s property. All rights-of-way are easements, but not all easements are rights-of-way, as easements can also cover purposes like the flow of utilities or support for structures. A right to use someone else’s land for a specific purpose without owning it. The scope of an easement can be broad, encompassing various uses such as installing utility lines, allowing support for a shared wall, or providing access to water.

A right-of-way is a legal easement that allows individuals to travel across or over another person’s property. It facilitates movement across land for a specific beneficiary or purpose. Easements can be for utilities, support, or access, whereas rights-of-way are exclusively for travel or passage. Easement is a broader category, while a right-of-way is a narrower, specific type of easement focused on access and passage.

In Illinois for example, renewable energy projects rely on private land easements and public right-of-way (ROW) agreements. Landowners typically negotiate voluntary lease and easement agreements with developers, often guided by the state’s Illinois Department of Agriculture and state setback rules to safeguard agricultural land and local drainage. Navigating these agreements in Illinois requires an understanding of several critical factors:

Agricultural Impact Mitigation Agreements (AIMAs)

Developers of large-scale wind and solar farms are required to negotiate an AIMA with the Illinois Department of Agriculture before construction. This ensures that productive farmland is restored to its pre-construction capabilities, protecting soil compaction, and securing just compensation for crop losses.

Easement Types

Projects require multiple easements. These include access easements for roads, utility easements for underground and overhead transmission lines, and “solar/wind rights” easements that prohibit adjacent property owners from building tall structures or planting trees that could block sunlight or wind.

Right-of-Way (ROW)

Developers frequently need to cross, occupy, or tie into public road rights-of-way. Under the state’s model ordinances, wind turbines are typically required to be set back at least 1.1 times their maximum blade tip height from the center point of public road rights-of-way.

State Setback & Drainage Protections

Recent legislation limits local control to reasonable regulations, meaning municipalities cannot outright ban renewable development. Facility owners are also authorized to cross drainage systems and drainage tiles during construction, provided they restore the land as soon as practicable.

Illinois offers just one example of the complexities of easements and rights-of-way. Always check with each state and county for comprehensive guidelines on hosting renewable infrastructure.

Undivided Interests

An undivided interest is a form of shared ownership where multiple parties hold rights to an entire property, rather than a specific portion of it. Each co-owner has an equal right to possess and enjoy the whole property, but their ownership is defined by a percentage share of the total asset. For instance, when three siblings inherit a property, each person possesses a one-third undivided interest in the entire residence. This means that all co-owners must agree to the terms of the lease or sale of the property.

Encumbrances and Liens

An encumbrance refers to a claim on a property that limits the owner’s ability to use it. A lien is a particular category of encumbrance, representing a financial claim intended to secure repayment of a debt. Encumbrances can also be non-financial, such as an easement that allows someone else to use a portion of the property, or a deed restriction that limits how the property can be developed. Claims by creditors or other parties against the property may need to be resolved or subordinated.

Probate Complications

Missing or unlocatable heirs are a significant complication in probate. The executor must locate and inform all beneficiaries before any assets can be distributed. This can lead to substantial delays, potentially stalling transactions for years and requiring extensive efforts like skip tracing. In probate, skip tracing means finding hard-to-locate beneficiaries, witnesses, or other parties to ensure they are properly notified or can provide needed information for administering the estate. This can be a complex and time-consuming process, especially for large or complex estates where family trees are extensive and members may be estranged or living in different jurisdictions. Probate complications impact mineral rights searches as well.

The court has a legal obligation to ensure all beneficiaries are notified of the probate proceedings. This requirement creates a mandatory pause in the process until all heirs are found or a reasonable effort to locate them has been exhausted.

Off-record Agreements

Unrecorded agreements related to property use such as grazing leases, water rights, or off-record access can create conflicts. These agreements may be legally binding between the parties involved, yet they lack public notice and can create issues for buyers or lenders who are unaware of the rights or restrictions, potentially leading to conflicting ownership claims or title defects.

Strategies for Unveiling and Addressing Complexities

Title Examination

Review the chain of title and all recorded documents. Identify all parties with a legal interest in the property. Engage real estate attorneys experienced in complex ownership issues who can interpret legal documents and advise on necessary actions.

Owner Communication

Directly contact owners to understand unrecorded interests or agreements. Use questionnaires to gather detailed information.

Verification of Authority

Obtain documentation proving individuals have the authority to sign, such as power of attorney, trustee certification or the like.

Negotiation and Resolution

Proactively address any disputes or claims. Negotiate terms that are acceptable to all parties.

Risks of Overlooking Ownership Complexities

Invalid Agreements: Missing consents can render leases unenforceable.

Project Delays: Time required to resolve issues can impact timelines.

Financial Implications: Additional costs for legal fees, settlements, or project redesign.

Warning

Real-World Example

A solar project was halted when a previously unknown easement allowed a utility company to install transmission lines across the property, conflicting with the project’s layout. The developer had to renegotiate with the utility, causing delays and additional costs.

Thoroughly investigating and addressing ownership complexities ensures that the project’s legal foundation is sound and reduces the risk of future disputes.
