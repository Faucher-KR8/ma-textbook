Chapter Five

Racing the Clock – Due Diligence Strategies for Tight Timelines

In the fast-paced world of mergers and acquisitions, companies often face tight deadlines to complete critical due diligence. It’s important to be thorough but efficient to ensure that no key issues are not overlooked. Typically, M&A transactions allow for a 60 to 90-day window for due diligence. Often, it is only a 60-day window, so you must do a quick analysis to understand what needs to be done.

In accelerated transaction environments, projects with organized documentation, clear site control, completed diligence, and well-structured data rooms often command greater market interest and move through financing and acquisition processes more efficiently than less-prepared assets. Pipeline prioritization is critical.

Data Room Readiness

Well-organized data rooms containing current title materials, surveys, permits, leases, environmental reports, and financing documentation can significantly accelerate acquisition review and improve buyer confidence during compressed transaction timelines.

An energy company successfully completed due diligence within a 60-day window by prioritizing critical leases and utilizing a specialized team to focus on high-risk areas, thereby avoiding potential delays in project development.

“Pre-Mortem” Risk Analysis

Some development teams conduct “pre-mortem” exercises in which stakeholders identify the most likely events that could prevent a project from achieving COD, financing milestones, or tax-credit qualification. These exercises help teams proactively prioritize mitigation strategies before risks become critical path issues.

Front-Loaded Diligence

In renewable energy transactions, unresolved mineral issues discovered late in the financing process can jeopardize closing timelines, increase curative costs, and create significant lender concerns. Successful projects rely on front-loaded diligence and proactive negotiation long before financing milestones are reached.

Experienced ALTA surveyors can play an important role during project closings by coordinating directly with title teams, responding to underwriting comments, revising certifications, and helping resolve boundary or access issues that arise during final financing review.

Local and regional experience matters significantly in renewable transactions. Surveyors and title professionals familiar with county-level practices, local access issues, historical land use patterns, and jurisdiction-specific requirements can often identify risks and resolve issues more efficiently than firms lacking regional expertise.

Government Agency Timing Risk and Transaction Delays

Compressed acquisition timelines create unique challenges when renewable energy projects involve state or local government land. Unlike private transactions, public-sector approvals often require multiple layers of internal review, legal oversight, and administrative coordination that do not always align with commercial financing schedules.

Developers frequently underestimate how significantly government processes can affect transaction timing. Even relatively straightforward agreements involving access easements, road crossings, school trust lands, water districts, or county-owned parcels may require review by agency counsel, governing boards, procurement departments, or state attorneys general before final approval can be granted. These review processes are rarely accelerated to accommodate financing deadlines or acquisition schedules.

Timing Risk is Often Administrative

Public agencies managing renewable energy requests are often balancing competing priorities with limited personnel and aging administrative systems. As a result, seemingly minor revisions to legal descriptions, insurance requirements, or agreement formatting can create unexpected delays late in the transaction process.

Projects involving checkerboard ownership patterns add another layer of complexity. Different parcels within the same project footprint may involve separate agencies, overlapping jurisdictions, or inconsistent review standards. Developers may complete diligence on privately controlled land only to discover that a single state-owned access corridor or utility crossing remains unresolved. Because these parcels often involve critical infrastructure or interconnection routes, unresolved government approvals can quickly become financing blockers.

To manage these risks effectively, developers should identify all government-controlled parcels early in the diligence process and prioritize approvals involving access roads, substations, collector systems, and transmission corridors. Public-sector approvals should be treated as critical path items rather than secondary administrative tasks.

Successful transaction teams also recognize the importance of relationship management during compressed timelines. Open communication with agency staff, title professionals, surveyors, and legal reviewers can help identify procedural issues early and prevent avoidable delays. In many cases, understanding how an agency operates internally can be just as important as understanding the legal requirements themselves.

Administrative review, agency coordination, and public-sector approval timelines can materially affect acquisition schedules, financing certainty, and commercial operation deadlines. Projects that anticipate these constraints early are far better positioned to maintain momentum and achieve successful closings.

Real World Considerations

Exclusivity agreements are commonly signed during fast-paced negotiations. The acquiring party must identify all issues within the set timeframe. The first thing that we recommend is to understand the intent of the transaction. What is the reason for acquiring the project? Is it market share? Is the aim to invest in this technology? Is it for cash flow? It is very important to understand the reason for the acquisition, because that will guide many of your transactions.

We recommend prioritizing key parcels or areas to manage tight time frames. Begin by identifying project improvements and locating your medium voltage. Where is your high voltage? Where is your interconnection? Where is your substation? Where is your access? Using that as a guide to identify key parcels is extremely important. It is also important to understand high risk issues as it relates to the client. This varies from project to project. M&A deals can happen early on. It’s just a matter of site control, but some clients want later stage projects. The risk profile varies depending on the stage of shovel-ready projects.

Managing Title Curative to Preserve Financing Timelines

Many developers utilize consolidated title review or “Red Light” reports to centralize title exceptions, survey encroachments, lease expirations, and unresolved curative items into a single tracking framework. These reports help transaction teams prioritize critical issues and coordinate cure strategies across legal, survey, title, and development teams.

Another important consideration is leveraging existing documents. Know what’s in the data room. Someone should be auditing the data room and making sure to note any gaps in the information. Ask for everything. This is a critical time to make sure that information is shared.

We recommend assembling a multidisciplinary team, the composition of the team will vary from project to project. Having the right experts, both internally and externally is critical. Ensure that everyone understands their responsibilities and that they communicate with one another so that no details are overlooked. Ensure they understand all relevant issues.

We are often tapped to be part of the team of experts who will be examining the title, looking at mineral rights, and looking at site control. When assembling your team, it is important that everyone understands who is responsible for each part of the due diligence. They need to know the critical deadlines and the focus of the work. Though it may seem simple, team members often lack clarity on key risk factors and when to share essential information during transactions. Make sure that there is regular communication with stakeholders, to review due diligence responsibilities and engage in problem solving.

Verifying Information is Critical

A clear example is a recent case where the seller was asked about a permitting issue, he said: “Don’t worry, it’s all taken care of. Not a problem.” Down the road, when project construction was about to begin, they discovered that the permitting issue had not been resolved. This caused unexpected delays and significant costs. Make sure to get all the information and documentation you need from the prospective seller, then conduct your own due diligence. Best practices will often standardize processes, so that everything is consistent, and everyone is informed.

Title review conducted under compressed transaction timelines carries significant underwriting risk. Public records may be incomplete, outdated, or inaccurate, and rushed diligence can result in overlooked lines, easements, mineral severances, or ownership defects. These omissions can create substantial downstream impact including title claims, financing delays, increased curative costs, and litigation exposure. Structured review procedures, experienced title professionals, and standardized diligence processes help reduce the likelihood of material issues being missed during fast-moving transactions.

Haste Makes Waste

  • Incomplete title searches create hidden transaction risks.
  • Time pressure increases the likelihood of overlooked encumbrances.
  • Public records may be inaccurate or incomplete.
  • Rushed diligence increases downstream litigation and claim exposure.
  • Experienced reviewers and standardized review procedures reduce risk.

Mineral Ownership Reports (MORs) should be ordered early in the diligence process, particularly in jurisdictions with active oil and gas development or historical severances. Mineral ownership analysis often requires extensive historical research and can materially impact underwriting timelines, financing certainty, and project layout decisions.

Strategies for Due Diligence Under Time Constraints - Prioritize Key Areas

  • Critical Parcels: Focus on land essential for project infrastructure.
  • High-Risk Issues: Identify areas with potential legal or environmental complications.

Examine Existing Documentation:

  • Review Sellers Data: Conduct a comprehensive review of all documentation provided by the seller.
  • Identify Gaps: Note missing or incomplete information for follow-up.

Identify Stakeholders

Landowners, residents, community groups, and government agencies are key stakeholders. Consider who is impacted by, interested in, or can influence the project.

Focus your initial efforts on landowners whose property is central to the project’s feasibility and potential construction footprint.

Engage with Landowners

To effectively engage landowners during renewable energy project due diligence, identify all relevant stakeholders, establish transparent and consistent communication through various channels and platforms. Inquire about any unrecorded agreements, mineral rights, and environmental concerns. Seek opportunities to build trust and to give landowners a sense of ownership and involvement in the project. Maintain consistency and follow-through.

Timing and Narrative Control

The earliest public narrative surrounding a project can significantly influence community perception and permitting outcomes. Delayed engagement or reactive communication strategies may allow opposition groups to frame the project negatively before developers establish trust with local stakeholders.

Establish Transparent Communication Channels

Employ a range of communication methods, including one-on-one meetings, community forums, online platforms and informational materials. Always be open and honest. Clearly communicate the project’s purpose, potential benefits, environmental considerations, and any potential impacts on land and community life.

We have seen so many projects where the developer has not controlled the messaging. If you don’t control the messaging for your project, others will and that could be a disaster. This is especially true with social media. Has anything about the project been posted on social media? Make sure you know what has been posted. Is the information truthful? If not, you will need to have conversations with stakeholders to discuss their concerns. Opposition to the project must be understood and included in your due diligence reporting or risk potential litigation.

Address Concerns Proactively

Provide clear information on how potential issues, such as noise, visual impact, or land access, will be mitigated during construction and operation.

Conduct Thorough Engagement Activities

Get feedback, use surveys, feedback forms, and open discussions to understand landowners’ specific needs, concerns, and perspectives regarding the project.

Document and Respond

Maintain comprehensive documentation of all communications and feedback, ensuring that commitments made to landowners are fulfilled and appropriately addressed.

Utilize Public Resources

  • Online Databases: Access property records, environmental data, and GIS maps.
  • Regulatory Agencies: Reach out to local authorities for zoning and permitting information.

Assemble a Multidisciplinary Team Including an ALTA Surveyor

The ALTA surveyor is a critical transaction partner in renewable energy development and project finance. ALTA surveys are frequently required for construction financing, tax equity transactions, title insurance endorsements, and final project closings. Selecting an experienced surveyor early in the diligence process can significantly improve coordination between developers, title companies, lenders, and underwriting teams while reducing closing delays. Use these criteria to select the right surveyor:

  • Familiarity with state/county requirements
  • Experience with renewable projects
  • Ability to meet financing timelines
  • Understanding Table A requirements
  • Ability to support title endorsements
  • Closing support experience
  • Coordination with title underwriters

Internal collaboration should include the legal, development, environmental, and engineering teams. External experts include consultants for specialized assessments.

Project financing transactions require close coordination between legal counsel, surveyors, title professionals, underwriting teams, and financial stakeholders. Misalignment among these groups can create delays, duplicate curative work, and unnecessary transaction risk.

Implement Technology Solutions

  • Data Management Tools: Use platforms that allow for efficient document review and collaboration.
  • Automated Alerts: Set up notifications for critical deadlines and tasks.

Managing Title Curative Under Tight Transaction Timelines

Title curative Is the linchpin of financing. It is one of the most important yet underestimated components of renewable energy M&A and project finance. Unresolved title defects can delay financing, trigger lender holdbacks, impact tax equity schedules, and jeopardize COD targets. Effective curative management requires a structured process for identifying, prioritizing, tracking, and resolving defects well before closing.

The A / B / C Defect Ranking System

Critical (A)

  • Financing blockers
  • Lease failures
  • Primary parcel defects
  • Title gaps

Important (B)

  • Lender holdback risks
  • Subordination issues
  • Moderate encroachments

Low Risk (C)

  • Long-tail risks
  • Peripheral parcels
  • Escrow candidates

Maximizing Efficiency

  • Standardized Processes: Develop checklists and templates to streamline tasks.
  • Regular Communication: Hold frequent team meetings to track progress and address issues promptly.
  • Delegate: Assign tasks based on expertise to ensure efficiency.
