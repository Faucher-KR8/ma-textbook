Chapter Six

Environmental and Site Considerations in Project Acquisitions

Environmental factors are critical in determining a project’s feasibility, impacting not only the site’s suitability but also regulatory compliance, community acceptance and long-term viability.

Environmental Site Assessment

An Environmental Site Assessment (ESA) for a renewable energy project is critical. The site assessment identifies potential environmental impacts and includes site inspections, reviews of historical records, government agency research, and analysis of factors such as biodiversity, land use, water resources, soil conditions, and local socio-economic impacts. The environmental site assessment also establishes the presence of hazardous materials and evaluates risks to human health and the environment, including water safety and air quality. An expert assessment ensures that potential negative impacts are identified, minimized or avoided before project development proceeds.

Key Components of an Environmental Site Assessment

An environmental site assessment involves a visual inspection of the site and a thorough review of property records to identify past land uses and potential environmental concerns. This includes the following:

Government Agency Records Research

Information is gathered from government agencies to identify regulatory requirements, environmental constraints, and records of environmental issues on or near the property.

Physical and Ecological Characteristics

The assessment evaluates the site’s suitability for the project, considering existing land uses, soil conditions, and topography.

Biological Environment

Impacts on wildlife, plants, and overall biodiversity are assessed.

Water Resources:

Water bodies, groundwater, and potential impacts on water quality are evaluated.

Air Quality

The project’s potential effects on local air quality are considered. For example, a California appeals court recently ruled that Kern County violated CEQA (California Environmental Quality Act) with a flawed environmental review for its oil and gas ordinance. Permitting was halted until the ordinance was brought into compliance. This underscores how local rules can override or delay development, even on privately held mineral estates.

Human Health

Potential impacts on human health and public safety are analyzed.

Cultural Heritage

Any effects on cultural heritage sites are assessed.

Community Impact

The assessment considers potential effects on the local community, including environmental justice issues.

Hazardous Materials and Contamination

The process identifies the presence of hazardous substances and assesses whether on-site contaminants pose health or environmental risks.

Risk Assessment

A comprehensive analysis of potential environmental risks is conducted to inform mitigation strategies.

Environmental Management Plan (EMP)

The findings are used to develop a plan for managing and mitigating environmental impacts during the project’s lifecycle, including decommissioning.

Key Factors that Can Derail a Project

Many factors impact project development and viability. Next, we’ll examine in greater detail key factors that can derail a project.

Flood Plains

Flooding can cause catastrophic damage to solar panels, inverters, and other electrical equipment, potentially leading to system failure and significant financial losses. Developing renewable energy projects in floodplains requires flood risk assessment involving site selection, flood-resilient design, and regulatory compliance to avoid damage, ensure worker safety, and protect natural flood mitigation services. Project development must consider elevated structures, flood-proof electrical components, and the potential impact of infrastructure on the natural flood control functions of floodplains, which are crucial for ecosystems and water management.

Floods occur naturally and can happen almost anywhere. They may not even be near a body of water, although river and coastal flooding are two of the most common types. Heavy rains, poor drainage, and nearby construction projects can put your project at risk for flood damage. Always look at FEMA maps. FEMA maintains and updates data through flood maps and risk assessments. Flood maps are one tool to know which areas have the highest risk of flooding. Once you understand the potential risk of flooding, you may need flood insurance.

Protected Species and Habitats

In addition to flood plains, there are other risks to examine. Determine early if protected species or habitats are present on site. Ask for biological assessments. Have wildlife agencies been contacted? Have they presented any data or recommendations to ensure legal compliance? If this information does not exist, contact relevant agencies to conduct a site review.

Warning

Unexpected issues can arise if environmental studies are not reviewed. For example, a wind energy project we worked on was delayed when it was discovered that the site was a migratory pathway for endangered birds. This meant that the developer had to implement significant mitigation measures, including curtailment strategies and habitat conservation plans, increasing costs and affecting timelines.

Wetlands and Water Bodies

Wetlands provide a multitude of ecological, economic, and social benefits. They supply habitats for diverse species, replenish groundwater, limit flooding, purify drinking water, yield food and fiber, and enable cultural and recreational activities. Projects affecting wetlands may need Clean Water Act permits.

Identify wetlands using National Wetlands Inventory (NWI) maps. The National Wetlands Inventory (NWI) was established by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) to conduct a nationwide inventory of U.S. wetlands to provide biologists, land management planners and others with information on the distribution and type of wetlands.

Soil and Geotechnical Conditions

Soil and geotechnical condition reports are essential for renewable energy projects, providing data on subsurface properties, soil strength, and potential hazards to ensure safe and stable foundation design for structures like wind turbines and solar arrays. These reports use field investigations (e.g., boreholes, CPT) and laboratory tests to determine soil characteristics, inform foundation selection, minimize risks, and guarantee the long-term stability and performance of the energy infrastructure.

Soil and geotechnical reports identify potential geotechnical hazards (e.g., slope stability, soil strength, ground instability) that could compromise the safety and longevity of the infrastructure. A thorough report helps understand the site-specific geological and subsurface conditions, including soil types, stratigraphy, and the presence of any anomalies. Evaluating ground stability for transmission lines and associated structures identifies potential ground settlement or other issues.

Cultural and Historical Sites

Cultural and historical sites are crucial considerations in renewable energy development because projects can impact historical sites, indigenous lands, and culturally significant landscapes. Can disturb or destroy archaeological sites containing artifacts, human remains, or historic structures. Many significant cultural and sacred sites are located on public or private land designated for renewable energy projects, leading to potential conflicts over land use and access. This can require archaeological surveys, community consultation, and impact assessments to avoid destruction and ensure respectful development practices.

Development on tribal lands requires compliance with federal, tribal, and state laws, and usually needs tribal or landowner approval. Federal agencies like the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) issue environmental permits for sources on tribal lands, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) handles leases and right-of-way grants. Tribes can often develop their own permitting programs and may need to meet specific criteria to qualify.

Tip

We once worked on a project in Wyoming where we conducted our own site review. We saw what looked to us like piles of rocks. Engaging local stakeholders revealed these were protected historical and cultural tribal artifacts. We had to negotiate with various groups, including tribal leaders and local landowners, to create easements that permit the tribes access to the sacred site to conduct their vision quests. Easements for all stakeholders to both the property and the project had to be designed. Understanding those rights and making sure that you deal with them early on is extremely important.

The visual character of a landscape can also be culturally significant. Large-scale wind turbines or extensive solar arrays can dramatically alter the aesthetic and historic qualities of a region, often leading to community opposition.

Health Protection Zones

We had a project in California where there are Health Protection Zones. Health Protection Zones are instituted by the county. These are large areas that do not allow any improvements. In California, “Health Protection Zones” (HPZs) are specific 3,200-foot buffer zones established around sensitive receptors (e.g., homes, schools, hospitals) intended to protect communities from industrial hazards. The 3,200 feet is measured from the property line of the sensitive receptor or the building footprint, depending on the setback distance. Rather than dictating general building practices, these zones strictly regulate the proximity and operations of the oil and gas industry.

Jurisdictions like Illinois have health protection zones within vicinity of the city. These factors are crucial, if you’re purchasing a 200 MW project or a 1000 MW portfolio, you don’t want to discover significant land use issues after land acquisition. That is another reason that environmental site assessments and community engagement are an important part of due diligence. Get to know the leaders and the influencers of the community, they can provide you with insights that can help you to more effectively identify potential issues.

Community Acceptance

Community acceptance has really moved up the ladder in terms of importance in transactions. Resistance to renewable energy projects stems from a wide range of concerns, including environmental impact, economic fears, and aesthetic objections. This is compounded by issues like a lack of early engagement, insufficient information, and distrust in developers or project governance. Overcoming this resistance requires early community dialogue, clear communication of project benefits and potential local gains, addressing specific concerns, and establishing long-term trust through local empowerment and commitment from developers and governing bodies.

Community engagement should be treated as a core component of project risk management rather than a secondary public relations function. Early engagement, consistent communication, and proactive stakeholder outreach can materially reduce permitting delays, litigation risk, and transaction uncertainty. Conduct online research and speak with community leaders to gain clarity and build support.

Navigating Tribal Lands and Sovereign Jurisdictions

Renewable energy development on tribal lands involves unique legal, regulatory, and governance considerations because federally recognized tribes operate as sovereign nations. Tribal lands are frequently held in trust by the federal government, and project development may require approvals, procedures, and enforceability considerations that differ substantially from conventional private-land transactions.

Tribal governance structures, leadership transitions, and community priorities may evolve during project development, making trust, transparency, and consistent engagement critical components of successful project execution.

Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) Approval Risk

Many tribal land agreements require post-execution approval from the Bureau of Indian Affairs. As a result, executed leases, assignments, easements, or surface agreements may not become legally effective until federal approval is obtained. Developers and acquirers should incorporate these additional approval timelines into project schedules, financing assumptions, and transaction risk assessments.

Opposition Risk Is a Material Development Risk

Community opposition has evolved into a significant project development and transaction risk within the renewable energy sector. In many jurisdictions, organized opposition groups have successfully delayed or blocked utility-scale renewable projects with political campaigns, public hearings, litigation threats, and social media activism. These risks can materially affect permitting certainty, development timelines, financing schedules, and project valuation.

Developers should be prepared to address misinformation, community concerns, and rapidly evolving public narratives surrounding renewable energy projects. Social media and organized opposition campaigns can accelerate public resistance if stakeholder engagement is delayed or inconsistent.

Stakeholder Segmentation

Developers should recognize that local communities are rarely homogeneous. Stakeholders often include landowners benefiting from lease revenue, local businesses expecting economic activity, government entities anticipating tax revenue, and residents who perceive little or no direct benefit from the project. Effective engagement strategies should be tailored to each group’s interests, concerns, and motivations.

Warning

Case Study – Migratory Birds

A wind energy project was delayed when it was discovered that the site was a migratory pathway for endangered birds. The developer had to implement mitigation measures, including curtailment strategies and habitat conservation plans, increasing costs and affecting timelines. Environmental due diligence and community engagement are not just about compliance but also about ensuring the long-term viability and acceptance of the project.
