Chapter 10: Inheriting a Project – Navigating the Challenges and Leveraging Opportunities

M&A is the key to filling pipeline gaps. With increasingly complex market and regulatory conditions, treat every renewable acquisition as if you’re buying development execution capability under extreme time pressure, not just development potential under favorable market conditions. This demands a holistic approach combining meticulous due diligence, strategic collaboration, and proactive risk management.

Acquiring existing projects offers a shortcut to market entry, but also presents unique challenges. At some point, every project will be the subject of a transaction — debt for Notice to Proceed (NTP) and start of construction, tax equity at operations, purchase or sale, whatever it might be. Ensuring everything is ready for financial closing requires thorough due diligence.

Fast-Transaction Readiness

Developers anticipating potential divestitures or accelerated sales should prepare projects for rapid diligence review by organizing title materials, surveys, permits, lease portfolios, interconnection documentation, and environmental records in advance. Market-ready projects transact more efficiently under compressed market conditions.

Due Diligence Checklist

  • Understanding Ownership Complexities: Identify all parties with interests in the property; ensure all necessary signatories are secured.
  • Navigating Regional Risks: Be aware of state-specific challenges like mineral rights; engage local experts for insights and solutions.
  • Efficient Time Management: Develop strategies to conduct diligence effectively under tight timelines; prioritize critical areas without sacrificing thoroughness.
  • Environmental Considerations: Incorporate environmental assessments from the outset; use public resources and specialist expertise.
  • Enhancing Team Collaboration: Foster communication between commercial and development teams; align objectives and incentives.
  • Accurate Valuation: Adjust valuations based on due diligence findings and realistic timelines.
  • Managing Inherited Projects: Conduct comprehensive audits of inherited work and plan for resource allocation to address challenges.

Challenges of Inheriting Projects

Challenge Risk
Unknown quality of prior work Incomplete or inadequate documentation, permitting issues, suboptimal design
Stakeholder relationships Existing relationships with landowners, regulators, and communities may not transfer smoothly — loss of goodwill or misunderstandings
Hidden issues Unidentified legal, environmental, or technical problems may surface later — unexpected costs and delays

The biggest handicap a development team can have is to inherit someone else’s documents and someone else’s work — because you don’t know what you are getting.

Advantages of Inheriting Projects

  • Accelerated Timelines: Bypassing early development stages brings projects online faster.
  • Cost Savings: Reduced expenditure on initial development activities.
  • Market Access: Immediate presence in desirable locations or markets.

Strategies for Managing Inherited Projects

  • Comprehensive Audit: Review all existing documentation thoroughly; identify gaps or areas requiring further work.
  • Resource Allocation: Plan for additional resources to address inherited issues; allocate budget for potential remediation or redesign.
  • Stakeholder Engagement: Reestablish relationships with landowners and authorities; clearly communicate ownership changes and future plans.
  • Risk Mitigation: Develop contingency plans for potential problems; consider purchasing insurance for specific risks.
  • Leverage Existing Assets: Utilize positive aspects of the previous developer’s work and build upon established community goodwill where possible.
Tip

Case Study Insight: Turning Opposition Into Support

A company acquired a wind farm project and discovered that community opposition had stalled permitting. By actively engaging with the community, addressing concerns, and making design adjustments, the company turned a challenging situation into a successful project.

Not Every Project Should Be Advanced

In some market environments, developers may determine that certain projects are unlikely to meet revised financing, permitting, or tax-credit timelines. In these situations, early monetization, partial divestiture, or strategic repositioning may preserve value more effectively than continued development. Rejecting a commercially promising project that exceeds your team’s capabilities to execute is, itself, a best practice.

Key Takeaways

  • Inheriting a project means inheriting someone else’s risk profile, documentation gaps, and stakeholder history — assume nothing until verified.
  • A comprehensive audit and deliberate resource allocation plan turn “unknown quality of prior work” into a manageable, budgeted risk.
  • Stakeholder relationships require active re-engagement; goodwill built by a previous developer does not automatically transfer.
  • Sometimes the best decision is to not advance a project — early monetization or repositioning can preserve more value than continued development under a deteriorating timeline.
