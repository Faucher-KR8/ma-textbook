Chapter 1: Navigating M&A Best Practices in Utility-Scale Renewable Energy Development

The renewable energy sector is evolving rapidly, with mergers and acquisitions (M&A) activities playing a pivotal role in shaping the industry’s future. This trend reflects the industry’s maturation and the increasing complexity of developing large-scale renewable projects.

This book will guide you through critical aspects of the M&A process, from verifying land control to fostering collaboration between teams. Successful M&A comes from checking many boxes. Sometimes, it is necessary to reject commercially promising projects if they exceed the development team’s capabilities. Due diligence, collaboration, and proactive risk management are essential for successful M&A outcomes.

Riding the Wave — Current M&A Trends in Renewable Energy

The renewable energy sector is experiencing a dynamic shift, with mergers and acquisitions becoming a cornerstone strategy for growth and expansion.

Why Companies Prefer M&A Over Greenfield Development

Greenfield development means beginning from the ground up — site selection, permitting, financing, and construction — which can take years. Acquiring existing projects offers distinct advantages:

  • Time Efficiency: Expedites operational readiness by eliminating initial development phases.
  • Risk Mitigation: Reduces exposure to development risks like permitting delays or community opposition.
  • Strategic Positioning: Allows companies to quickly enter new markets or technologies.
  • Resource Optimization: Enables better allocation of resources by focusing on operational efficiency rather than development hurdles.

Solar projects typically require five to seven years, while wind projects can take seven to ten years — not including permitting on public lands, which can involve stakeholders such as the Bureau of Land Management or the Bureau of Indian Affairs. Securing land rights may be significantly prolonged when numerous stakeholders are engaged. M&A lets you skip the initial development phase, saving a great deal of time and risk.

M&A Projects Span a Wide Spectrum of Development Stages

  • Pre-construction projects possess land leases and may have obtained necessary permits; construction has not yet commenced.
  • Under-construction projects: Physical construction has started, reducing uncertainty around project realization.
  • Operational projects: Already generating energy and revenue, providing immediate cash flow.

Market Drivers Behind the M&A Surge

  • Regulatory Incentives: Policies like the Investment Tax Credit (ITC) and Production Tax Credit (PTC) incentivize timely project completion.
  • Market Demand: Corporations and utilities are increasing renewable energy procurement to meet sustainability goals.
  • Financial Markets: Liquidity and interest-rate conditions shape the attractiveness of acquisition financing.
  • Technological Maturity: Advances in technology reduce operational risks, making existing projects more valuable.

Policy Context: The 2025–2026 Shift

The termination and restriction of certain Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) tax credits under the “One Big Beautiful Bill” (OBBB), enacted July 4, 2025, has reshaped the M&A landscape. The law alters or eliminates certain IRA tax credits, adds Foreign Entity of Concern (FEOC) restrictions to remaining credits, and an accompanying Executive Order directs Treasury to tighten the definition of “beginning of construction.” The elimination of commonly used safe harbors has thrown eligibility standards into flux. Analysts estimate that up to 29 GW of wind and solar projects could fail to meet tightened deadlines, with as much as 122 GW and $211 billion in investment at risk from the combined effect of phase-outs, tariffs, and regulatory constraints.

The result: a divide between projects that locked in credits early and those now struggling under new rules, increased deal-making as companies navigate valuation pressure, and a push toward more risk-hedged, structured financing.

Despite policy reversals, high electricity demand — especially from data centers and electrification — continues to support the business case for renewables. Projects with safe-harbored tax credits retain value; others face steep discounting. Investors are increasingly favoring “boring but bankable” assets — projects with proven cash flows and minimal exposure to policy-related disruption — over speculative, subsidy-reliant technologies. Rather than straightforward equity rounds, stakeholders are turning to layered financing — project finance, debt instruments, preferred equity, and recapitalizations — to avoid diluting ownership and remain attractive to potential acquirers.

Why This Matters: The Cost of Getting It Wrong

About 40% of M&A deals fail, often due to overlooked details discovered only at closing. Land is a fundamental asset for renewable projects — it is imperative to verify land control to ensure that the project rests on solid ground, literally and legally. Land issues can delay deals, increase legal costs, and disrupt timelines just before closing.

Although the primary emphasis of this book is on mergers and acquisitions, the expertise, insights, and strategies presented throughout are relevant across all stages of project development — whether you are an industry veteran or new to the field.

Industry Observations

At a recent conference, discussions centered on M&A strategies, with many companies now interested in acquiring projects past the initial development stage — a strategic shift toward portfolios that meet market demands and investor expectations quickly. Companies considering M&A must navigate complex due diligence processes to ensure successful acquisitions.

Key Takeaways

  • M&A activity is surging as companies respond to valuation pressure, policy disruption, and the relative speed of acquisition versus greenfield development.
  • Up to 29 GW of wind and solar projects may miss tightened tax-credit deadlines under post-OBBB rules; up to 122 GW and $211 billion in investment is potentially at risk.
  • About 40% of M&A deals fail, often due to issues only discovered at closing — almost always tied to land, ownership, or undisclosed risk.
  • Projects span pre-construction, under-construction, and operational stages, each carrying a different risk and valuation profile.
  • “Boring but bankable” assets with proven cash flow are increasingly preferred over speculative, subsidy-dependent technology bets.
