Chapter 3: Ensuring Correct Ownership – Getting the Signatures Right

Why Ownership Structure Matters

Every renewable energy project is owned by a legal entity. That entity may be a simple limited liability company (LLC), a complex master limited partnership (MLP), a real estate investment trust (REIT), or a multinational corporation’s subsidiary.

In an M&A transaction, the buyer is not acquiring the physical project—the land, the panels, the turbines. The buyer is acquiring ownership of the entity that owns the project. This fundamental distinction shapes the entire transaction.

Getting ownership right means:

  1. Understanding the target entity structure: Who owns the entity? Are there multiple classes of equity? Are there hidden claims or liens against equity?
  2. Confirming title to equity: The sellers actually own what they claim to own, and they have authority to sell.
  3. Identifying all claimants: Are there debt instruments, preferred equity, options, or other claims that must be resolved before transfer?
  4. Resolving conflicts: If multiple parties claim ownership, settle those claims before closing.
  5. Documenting the transfer: Execute clean, legally binding documents that transfer all ownership rights.

This chapter addresses the structure, verification, and transfer of project ownership.

Ownership Structure Types

1. Simple LLC

Many smaller renewable projects are owned by a single-purpose limited liability company (LLC). The LLC is formed under state law and owns the project. Members (owners) hold ownership interests in the LLC, typically represented by membership units.

Advantages: - Simple structure - Clear ownership documentation - Flexible governance

Risks in M&A: - If multiple members exist, all must consent to sale - Membership agreements may restrict transferability - Operating agreements may require “call” or “put” options, limiting sellers’ ability to exit

2. Partnerships and Joint Ventures

Some projects are owned by partnerships—either general partnerships (GPs) or limited partnerships (LPs). In a partnership structure:

  • General Partners (GPs) manage the entity and are personally liable for debts
  • Limited Partners (LPs) are passive investors with limited liability

The partnership agreement specifies each partner’s ownership percentage, profit sharing, and transfer rights.

Risks in M&A: - If multiple partners exist, all must consent to sale (unless partnership agreement permits GP to sell on behalf of all partners) - Partner buyouts, call options, or put options may complicate the transaction - If a partner dies, estate issues may delay transfer

3. Corporate Ownership

Some projects are owned by corporations or subsidiaries of larger companies. Ownership is evidenced by stock certificates.

Risks in M&A: - If multiple shareholders exist, all must consent (or a supermajority, depending on bylaws) - Stock purchase agreements may contain vesting schedules, restricting founder ability to sell - Tax considerations: purchasing stock is treated differently from purchasing assets

4. Complex Ownership Structures

Large renewable platforms often use complex structures:

Tiered Ownership: A parent company (often a REIT or MLP) owns multiple subsidiaries, each owning one or more projects. The buyer may acquire subsidiary equity, parent company equity, or both.

Preferred Equity Tranches: Some platforms issue preferred equity—shares with priority claims on distributions—alongside common equity. A preferred shareholder has greater security but lower upside.

Earnout Structures: Sale prices may be contingent on future performance (e.g., "base price of $50M plus $5M if the project exceeds 90% capacity factor for three years"). These complicate transaction closure and post-closing dispute resolution.

Stock Purchase vs. Asset Purchase

In M&A transactions, the buyer can structure the acquisition as either a stock/equity purchase or an asset purchase.

Stock/Equity Purchase

The buyer purchases 100% of the outstanding equity of the target entity. The entity itself (with all its contracts, properties, and liabilities) transfers to the buyer.

Advantages: - Simpler structure; fewer contracts require assignment - Clear continuation of entity (contracts don’t break) - Faster closing

Disadvantages: - Buyer inherits all historical liabilities (environmental, labor, contractual) - Buyer may inherit unknown claims or defects - Tax treatment may be less favorable (ordinary income vs. capital gains)

Asset Purchase

The buyer purchases specific assets (land lease, PPA contract, physical equipment) from the target entity. The target entity remains with the seller, and the seller retains any residual liabilities.

Advantages: - Buyer does not inherit unknown liabilities - Tax treatment may be more favorable - Buyer can negotiate what assets to acquire and what to leave behind

Disadvantages: - Requires assignment of each contract (lease, PPA, O&M, insurance) - More complex; more likely that contracts cannot be assigned - May require third-party consents

Common Ownership Issues in M&A

Issue 1: Multiple Equity Holders with Conflicting Interests

A project LLC has two equal members: Developer A and Investor B. Developer A wants to sell the project, but Investor B wants to hold it for five more years.

Solution: Review the operating agreement. If it requires unanimous consent for sale, the transaction cannot proceed without Investor B’s agreement. If it permits sale by majority vote, Developer A might have to offer Investor B a “tag-along” right (the right to sell alongside the founder).

Issue 2: Lien Against Equity

A bank financed the project with a debt secured by (a) the land lease, (b) the PPA contract, and (c) all equity in the project LLC. The debt must be paid off at closing before the equity can transfer to the buyer.

Solution: Coordinate with the lender to ensure the debt payoff happens simultaneously with equity transfer. Use escrow to hold funds.

Issue 3: Founder’s Equity Subject to Vesting

A renewable developer issued founder’s shares with a 4-year vesting schedule. Year 1 founder has only 25% vesting, meaning 75% of founder’s shares are still subject to forfeiture if the founder departs.

Solution: If the founder intends to sell, vesting schedules must be resolved beforehand. Some transactions accelerate vesting as part of the sale.

Best Practices for Ownership Verification

  1. Understand the Structure: Before beginning diligence, create a clear cap table showing all equity holders, ownership percentages, and any special rights or preferences.

  2. Obtain Resolutions: Secure board resolutions or member resolutions authorizing the sale. These should be specific to the buyer and transaction at hand.

  3. Search for Hidden Claims: Conduct UCC, judgment lien, and tax lien searches on the entity. Any creditor or judgment holder could block the transaction if not resolved.

  4. Verify Authority: Confirm that all signatories to the purchase agreement have actual authority to bind the entity. This is especially important in tiered structures.

  5. Secure Third-Party Consents: If debt, preferred equity, or other instruments require consent for sale, obtain written consents before closing.

  6. Document the Transfer: Ensure share certificates or membership interests are clean and free of liens or restrictions. Execute clear transfer documents.

  7. Choose Structure Wisely: Work with your tax and legal advisors to determine whether a stock or asset purchase is more favorable for your transaction.

Key Takeaways

  • In M&A, the buyer acquires ownership of the entity that owns the project, not the physical assets directly.
  • Ownership structure varies from simple LLCs to complex tiered partnerships and preferred equity tranches.
  • Verification of ownership requires understanding entity formation, capitalization, authority to sell, and any liens or claims against equity.
  • Hidden equity holders, vesting schedules, preferred equity, and lender consents are common obstacles to closing.
  • Stock vs. asset purchase structure has different tax and liability implications; choose based on transaction specifics and tax advice.
