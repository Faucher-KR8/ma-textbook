Chapter 9: Accurately Assessing Worth and Timing in Renewable Energy M&A

Accurate valuation is a fundamental component of successful M&A transactions. The Commercial Operation Date (COD) is critical in evaluating renewable project acquisitions because it signifies the point at which the project is fully operational, grid-connected, and ready to produce energy under its Power Purchase Agreement (PPA). COD triggers major financial events: release of final project funding, the off-taker’s obligation to purchase power at the contracted rate, and the transition from construction to operational security. An earlier COD can increase valuation by accelerating revenue and decreasing project risk.

Why COD Matters for Acquisitions

Revenue Generation Trigger: COD marks the start of revenue-generating activity, as the off-taker becomes contractually obligated to purchase power under the PPA.

Financing Milestones: COD often aligns with the release of remaining project funding and tax equity investment. Delays in survey completion, unresolved title matters, or unmet ALTA endorsement requirements can directly impact financing timelines, construction loan closings, and tax equity transactions — coordinate survey, title, underwriting, and financing teams early to avoid last-minute closing disruptions.

Performance Guarantees: COD sets the baseline for post-operation performance guarantees, including energy output, which can trigger penalties if not met.

PPA Security Transition: The PPA’s financial security requirements shift from construction-period security to post-commercial-operation security at COD, affecting the asset’s financial profile and valuation.

Tax Equity Closings and Title Readiness

In tax-equity transactions, site control is one of the most critical components of financing certainty. Tax equity investors, lenders, and underwriting teams require clear evidence that lease portfolios, easements, rights-of-way, and title documentation fully support long-term operations and tax-credit qualification timelines. Even minor title defects or unresolved survey issues can delay funding or jeopardize ITC/PTC qualification.

Developers and tax-equity investors frequently use title-risk reserves or targeted escrows to manage unresolved defects while preserving financing schedules. Structured release mechanisms tied to curative milestones help align incentives between sponsors, investors, title underwriters, and lenders while limiting open-ended exposure.

Project De-Risking and Valuation Before/After COD

Projects sold before COD are essentially “developer fee” transactions, where value derives from completed development milestones; the price reflects development work and future revenue potential. Projects at or near COD are significantly de-risked and valued on proven cash flow and operational performance, making them more attractive to investors.

Note

“Project Slippage” as a Valuation Risk

Buyers and investors increasingly evaluate “project slippage” risk when assessing renewable energy portfolios. Delays involving permitting, title curative, interconnection, mineral rights, or community opposition may materially reduce the likelihood that projects meet financing and construction deadlines — directly affecting valuation and marketability. Minor title defects, survey inconsistencies, or lease discrepancies discovered late in financing can create disproportionate impacts, including lender holdbacks, delayed funding, increased curative costs, and threats to ITC/PTC timelines.

Lease Expiration vs. COD/PTC Timing

Developers should evaluate lease expiration schedules against anticipated financing milestones, COD targets, and tax-credit qualification deadlines. A lease lapse prior to financing or commercial operation can create material transaction and valuation risk — overlay lease calendars against COD/PTC timelines and treat unresolved extensions as a flag, not a footnote.

The Impact of Timing on Valuation

  • COD Targets: Pivotal in determining a project’s value, especially when aligned with market demand or regulatory incentives.
  • Market Conditions: Energy prices, technology costs, and policy environments fluctuate, affecting project economics.

Developers should periodically conduct strategic pipeline reviews to identify which projects are most likely to achieve COD within required financing or tax-credit timelines. Projects facing significant title, permitting, interconnection, community, or site-control risk may require restructuring, acceleration, or monetization before market conditions change.

Warning

Policy Changes Compress M&A Timelines. Legislative and policy changes can suddenly compress the entire transaction market. Tax credit revisions and evolving “start construction” requirements can rapidly compress development and M&A timelines, increasing pressure to advance projects into construction, accelerate financing, or monetize assets quickly before incentive qualification windows narrow.

Adjusting Valuation Based on Due Diligence

  • Risk Assessment: Quantify risks identified during diligence (legal, environmental) and adjust the discount rate or required return accordingly.
  • Underwriting Deficiencies: Unclear policy language creates litigation risk.
  • Cost Implications: Estimate additional costs to resolve issues (legal fees, remediation) and incorporate them into the financial model.
  • Timing Delays: Reassess cash flow projections for potential delays and the impact on deadline-tied incentives.

Permitting risk and community opposition should be incorporated into project risk assessments and valuation models — evaluate public sentiment, local political dynamics, prior permitting history, and evidence of organized opposition when assessing timelines and financial exposure.

Valuation Methodologies

  • Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): Projects future cash flows and discounts them to present value.
  • Comparable Transactions: Analyzes similar deals to gauge market value.
  • Current Market Conditions: Tax incentives, interest rates, and related market conditions.
  • Replacement Cost: The cost to replicate the project from scratch.

Negotiation Strategies

  • Price Adjustments: Negotiate purchase price reductions to account for identified issues.
  • Earn-Outs: Structure deals where part of the payment is contingent on achieving milestones.
  • Indemnities and Warranties: Include contractual protections against specific risks.

Cost, Risk & Residual Risk Management

A comprehensive document audit at the outset reveals potential risk factors — every issue can be boiled down to a cost, and once a cost is assigned, a mitigation strategy (insurance or another indemnity instrument) can be developed. Not all title defects require immediate physical resolution before closing; developers and lenders may use escrows, endorsements, indemnities, or structured underwriting solutions to manage residual risk while preserving financing timelines.

Tip

We recently closed a rare case where we had to file a title claim on behalf of a client and were successful in obtaining coverage for the legal costs involved in resolving the issue — the title company ultimately covered the legal resolution costs. Title insurance remains one of the most effective tools for identifying and mitigating risk that survives even the most thorough diligence.

Tribal land transactions may involve additional financing and enforceability considerations — sovereign immunity, federal trust structures, and jurisdictional limitations can affect lender remedies, collateral rights, and dispute resolution. Engage experienced legal counsel and title professionals early when evaluating tribal land projects.

Warning

Case Study Insight

A company acquired a project expecting COD in 2026. Due diligence revealed permitting delays that could push COD to 2028, significantly affecting the project’s net present value. The company renegotiated the purchase price to reflect the new timeline and associated risks.

Key Takeaways

  • COD is the single most important inflection point in renewable project valuation — it converts development risk into operational cash flow.
  • Tax equity and lender requirements turn title and survey readiness into a financing-critical workstream, not a legal afterthought.
  • “Project slippage” — cumulative delay risk across permitting, title, interconnection, and community factors — should be explicitly priced into valuation models.
  • Escrows, endorsements, and title-risk reserves let transactions close around unresolved defects without abandoning financing timelines.
  • Failed or poorly managed projects create reputational impact beyond a single developer — communities often view renewable projects collectively, making responsible practices an industry-wide imperative.

Discussion Questions

  2. How would you structure a title-risk reserve to satisfy both a sponsor’s need to close on schedule and a lender’s need for protection?
  3. What due diligence findings would justify a price adjustment versus an earn-out structure?
