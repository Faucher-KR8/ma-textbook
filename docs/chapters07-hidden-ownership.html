Chapter 7: Hidden Layers – Addressing Complex Ownership Structures in Project Acquisitions

M&A has become one of the fastest ways to fill pipeline gaps in the renewable sector. With interconnection queues stretching for years and greenfield development slowing under regulatory and community pressure, acquiring projects can be the most effective path forward. But there’s a catch: deals that look strong on paper can fall apart at closing. The reason is almost always the same — risk buried in the details, such as land ownership issues or missing signatures that weren’t uncovered early enough.

Every deal carries risk. The question is whether you identify those risks early, when they can be managed, or later, when they threaten valuation and delay closing.

Hidden Ownership Complexities

Ownership isn’t always straightforward. Overlooking a single signatory can render a lease unenforceable; unrecorded rights, which often exist, can completely derail financing. Common issues include:

  • Life Estates: If the life tenant hasn’t signed, the agreement is incomplete.
  • Trusts & Estates: Require specific authorization to bind the entity.
  • Easements and Rights-of-Way: Allow third parties to use parts of the property, potentially limiting development or requiring adjustments.
  • Undivided Interests: All co-owners must consent to lease terms.
  • Encumbrances and Liens: Claims by creditors or other parties that need to be resolved or subordinated.
  • Probate Complications: Missing heirs can stall transactions for years.
  • Off-Record Agreements: Grazing leases, water rights, or off-record access can create conflicts.

It is critical to look beyond the deed — confirm signatory authority and identify unrecorded interests through landowner conversations. Don’t assume the title report tells the whole story.

Life Estates

A life estate is a legal ownership interest lasting for the duration of the “life tenant’s” life, dividing ownership into two interests: the right to possess and use the property during the tenant’s lifetime, and the right to receive the property after death (the “remainderman”). The life tenant cannot leave the property to heirs — their interest ends automatically at death. For example, a deed reading “To John Doe for life, then to Jane Doe” gives John a life estate; upon his death, Jane automatically receives full ownership. The life tenant’s consent is required for any lease or agreement affecting their interest, so their signature must be on all related agreements.

Trusts & Estates

A trust is a legal arrangement to manage and distribute assets, moving ownership from the original owner to the trust. Three stakeholders are typically involved: the grantor (who places assets into the trust), the trustee (who manages the assets for the beneficiaries’ benefit), and the beneficiary (who receives the assets once trust conditions are met). Trusts may be revocable (changeable by the grantor while alive) or irrevocable (difficult or impossible to change once established). Establishing a trust allows the grantor and beneficiaries to avoid probate, which can cause major delays because all heirs must sign documents related to transactions. Trust property requires trustee action and verification of both their authority and the trust’s terms.

Easements and Rights-of-Way

An easement is a legal right for a non-owner to use another’s land for a specific purpose; a right-of-way is a specific type of easement granting the right to travel or pass across property. All rights-of-way are easements, but not all easements are rights-of-way — easements can also cover utility lines or support for structures.

Note

Illinois Example: Easements in Practice

In Illinois, renewable energy projects rely on private land easements and public right-of-way agreements, with landowners typically negotiating voluntary lease and easement agreements guided by the state’s Department of Agriculture and setback rules protecting agricultural land and drainage.

  • Agricultural Impact Mitigation Agreements (AIMAs): Developers of large-scale wind and solar farms must negotiate an AIMA with the Illinois Department of Agriculture before construction, ensuring farmland is restored to pre-construction condition and crop losses are compensated.
  • Easement Types: Access easements for roads, utility easements for transmission lines, and “solar/wind rights” easements that prohibit adjacent owners from building structures or planting trees that block sunlight or wind.
  • Right-of-Way: Developers frequently need to cross or tie into public road rights-of-way; under model ordinances, wind turbines are typically set back at least 1.1 times their maximum blade tip height from the center point of public road rights-of-way.
  • State Setback & Drainage Protections: Recent legislation limits local control to reasonable regulations — municipalities cannot outright ban renewable development — and facility owners are authorized to cross drainage systems during construction, provided they restore the land as soon as practicable.

Illinois is just one example. Always check each state and county for its own easement and right-of-way requirements.

Undivided Interests

An undivided interest is a form of shared ownership where multiple parties hold rights to an entire property rather than a specific portion — each co-owner possesses an equal right to the whole, defined by a percentage share. For instance, three siblings who inherit a property each hold a one-third undivided interest in the entire residence, and all co-owners must agree to lease or sale terms.

Encumbrances and Liens

An encumbrance is a claim on a property limiting the owner’s ability to use it; a lien is a specific type of encumbrance representing a financial claim securing repayment of debt. Encumbrances can also be non-financial — an easement allowing someone else to use part of the property, or a deed restriction limiting development. Claims by creditors or other parties may need to be resolved or subordinated before closing.

Probate Complications

Missing or unlocatable heirs are a significant complication in probate — the executor must locate and inform all beneficiaries before assets can be distributed, leading to delays that can stall transactions for years and require extensive skip tracing (finding hard-to-locate beneficiaries or witnesses). This can be especially complex for large estates with extensive family trees or estranged or geographically dispersed members. The court has a legal obligation to ensure all beneficiaries are notified, creating a mandatory pause until all heirs are found or a reasonable effort to locate them has been exhausted. Probate complications affect mineral rights searches as well.

Off-Record Agreements

Unrecorded agreements related to property use — grazing leases, water rights, off-record access — may be legally binding between the original parties, yet lack public notice. This creates issues for buyers or lenders unaware of the rights or restrictions, potentially leading to conflicting ownership claims or title defects.

Strategies for Unveiling and Addressing Complexities

Title Examination: Review the chain of title and all recorded documents; identify all parties with a legal interest. Engage real estate attorneys experienced in complex ownership issues.

Owner Communication: Directly contact owners to understand unrecorded interests or agreements; use questionnaires to gather detailed information.

Verification of Authority: Obtain documentation proving individuals have authority to sign — power of attorney, trustee certification, or similar.

Negotiation and Resolution: Proactively address disputes or claims and negotiate terms acceptable to all parties.

Warning

Real-World Example

A solar project was halted when a previously unknown easement allowed a utility company to install transmission lines across the property, conflicting with the project’s layout. The developer had to renegotiate with the utility, causing delays and additional costs.

Key Takeaways

  • The deed is the starting point of ownership verification, not the finish line — life estates, trusts, easements, and off-record rights routinely sit beneath the surface.
  • Probate and missing-heir complications can stall a transaction for years; skip tracing and court notification requirements cannot be compressed to fit a deal timeline.
  • Easement and right-of-way requirements (like Illinois’s AIMA process) are jurisdiction-specific and must be researched state by state and county by county.
  • Risks identified early can be managed; risks discovered late threaten valuation and closing.
