Chapter 6: Environmental and Site Considerations in Project Acquisitions

📖 You're reading the condensed, interactive edition. View the full verbatim manuscript mirror →

Environmental factors are critical in determining a project’s feasibility, impacting site suitability, regulatory compliance, community acceptance, and long-term viability.

Environmental Site Assessment (ESA)

An ESA identifies potential environmental impacts through site inspections, historical records review, government agency research, and analysis of biodiversity, land use, water resources, soil conditions, and local socio-economic impacts. It establishes the presence of hazardous materials and evaluates risks to human health and the environment, including water safety and air quality. An expert assessment ensures negative impacts are identified, minimized, or avoided before development proceeds.

Key components include: government agency records research; physical and ecological characteristics; biological environment; water resources; air quality; human health; cultural heritage; community impact; hazardous materials and contamination; risk assessment; and an Environmental Management Plan (EMP) that governs mitigation through decommissioning.

A California appeals court recently ruled that Kern County violated CEQA (California Environmental Quality Act) with a flawed environmental review for its oil and gas ordinance — permitting was halted until the ordinance was brought into compliance. This underscores how local rules can override or delay development, even on privately held mineral estates.

Key Factors That Can Derail a Project

Flood Plains

Flooding can cause catastrophic damage to solar panels, inverters, and other electrical equipment. Developing in floodplains requires flood risk assessment covering site selection, flood-resilient design, and regulatory compliance — elevated structures, flood-proof electrical components, and attention to the natural flood mitigation services floodplains provide. Floods can occur almost anywhere, not only near water bodies; heavy rains, poor drainage, and nearby construction can create risk. Always check FEMA flood maps, and secure flood insurance once risk is understood.

Protected Species and Habitats

Determine early whether protected species or habitats are present. Request biological assessments and confirm whether wildlife agencies have been contacted and have offered recommendations for legal compliance.

Case Study: Migratory Birds

A wind energy project we worked on was delayed when it was discovered that the site sat on a migratory pathway for endangered birds. The developer had to implement significant mitigation measures — including curtailment strategies and habitat conservation plans — increasing costs and affecting timelines. Environmental due diligence and community engagement are not just about compliance; they protect the long-term viability and acceptance of the project.

Wetlands and Water Bodies

Wetlands supply habitat, replenish groundwater, limit flooding, purify drinking water, and enable cultural and recreational activities. Projects affecting wetlands may need Clean Water Act permits — identify wetlands using National Wetlands Inventory (NWI) maps, established by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to provide biologists, planners, and others with data on wetland distribution and type.

Soil and Geotechnical Conditions

Soil and geotechnical reports provide subsurface data needed for safe, stable foundation design — using boreholes, CPT, and laboratory testing to determine soil characteristics, inform foundation selection, and guarantee long-term stability. These reports identify hazards such as slope instability or ground settlement that could affect turbines, arrays, or transmission structures.

Cultural and Historical Sites

Projects can impact historical sites, indigenous lands, and culturally significant landscapes, potentially disturbing archaeological sites containing artifacts, human remains, or historic structures. This often requires archaeological surveys, community consultation, and impact assessments. Development on tribal lands requires compliance with federal, tribal, and state law, and usually needs tribal or landowner approval — the EPA issues environmental permits for sources on tribal lands, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) handles leases and right-of-way grants. Tribes can develop their own permitting programs subject to specific qualifying criteria.

Case Study: Sacred Sites in Wyoming

We once worked on a project in Wyoming where our own site review revealed what looked like piles of rocks. Engaging local stakeholders revealed these were protected historical and cultural tribal artifacts. We negotiated with tribal leaders and local landowners to create easements permitting tribes access to the sacred site for vision quests — easements covering both the property and the project had to be designed for all stakeholders. Understanding those rights, and addressing them early, was essential to keeping the project moving.

The visual character of a landscape can also be culturally significant — large-scale wind turbines or extensive solar arrays can dramatically alter the aesthetic and historic qualities of a region, often leading to community opposition.

Health Protection Zones

In California, “Health Protection Zones” (HPZs) are 3,200-foot buffer zones established by counties around sensitive receptors (homes, schools, hospitals) to protect communities from industrial hazards, measured from the property line or building footprint depending on setback distance. Rather than dictating general building practices, these zones strictly regulate the proximity and operations of the oil and gas industry. Other jurisdictions, including parts of Illinois, maintain their own health protection zones near cities. If you’re purchasing a 200 MW project — or a 1,000 MW portfolio — you don’t want to discover significant land use issues after acquisition. Get to know community leaders and influencers; they can flag potential issues long before formal review.

Community Acceptance

Community acceptance has moved up the ladder of importance in transactions. Resistance stems from environmental impact concerns, economic fears, and aesthetic objections, compounded by a lack of early engagement, insufficient information, and distrust of developers or project governance. Overcoming resistance requires early dialogue, clear communication of project benefits and local gains, addressing specific concerns directly, and establishing long-term trust through local empowerment and developer commitment.

Community engagement should be treated as a core component of project risk management, not a secondary public relations function. Early engagement, consistent communication, and proactive outreach can materially reduce permitting delays, litigation risk, and transaction uncertainty.

Navigating Tribal Lands and Sovereign Jurisdictions

Renewable energy development on tribal lands involves unique legal, regulatory, and governance considerations because federally recognized tribes operate as sovereign nations. Tribal lands are frequently held in trust by the federal government, and development may require approvals and enforceability considerations that differ substantially from conventional private-land transactions. Tribal governance structures, leadership transitions, and community priorities may evolve during development, making trust, transparency, and consistent engagement essential to successful execution.

Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) Approval Risk:

Many tribal land agreements require post-execution approval from the BIA — executed leases, assignments, easements, or surface agreements may not become legally effective until federal approval is obtained. Build these additional approval timelines into project schedules, financing assumptions, and transaction risk assessments.

Opposition Risk Is a Material Development Risk

Organized opposition groups have successfully delayed or blocked utility-scale renewable projects through political campaigns, public hearings, litigation threats, and social media activism — materially affecting permitting certainty, development timelines, financing schedules, and project valuation. Developers should be prepared to address misinformation and rapidly evolving public narratives; social media and organized opposition can accelerate resistance if engagement is delayed or inconsistent.

Local communities are rarely homogeneous: landowners benefiting from lease revenue, local businesses expecting economic activity, government entities anticipating tax revenue, and residents who perceive little direct benefit will all respond differently. Effective engagement strategies should be tailored to each group’s interests, concerns, and motivations.

Key Takeaways

  • An Environmental Site Assessment is not a formality — it is the basis for permitting, financing, and long-term operational compliance, including decommissioning planning.
  • Tribal land, cultural heritage, and BIA approval risk demand a government-to-government relationship mindset rather than a conventional transactional approach.
  • Community opposition is a material, quantifiable transaction risk, not a soft public relations issue — and it should be priced into valuation and timeline assumptions.
  • Local zoning innovations like California’s Health Protection Zones can override or delay development even where mineral or land rights are otherwise clear.
  • Controlling the narrative early, and treating community stakeholders as differentiated groups rather than a monolith, materially reduces permitting and litigation risk.
