6  Chapter 2: Verifying Land Control – The Foundation of Acquisition


## Why Land Control Matters

Land is the irreplaceable foundation of every renewable energy project. A solar array without land is imagination. Wind turbines without properly controlled land are financial fiction.

Verifying that a developer—or the target of an acquisition—actually *controls* the land on which a project sits is the first and most critical step in M&A due diligence. Yet it is also the step most often compressed, delayed, or assigned to junior staff without sufficient supervision.

The consequences of inadequate land verification are severe:

- **Deals collapse** at closing because a lessor refuses to consent to assignment.
- **Projects fail** after acquisition because the previous developer's leases were non-assignable or contained undisclosed termination clauses.
- **Financing is withdrawn** because lenders cannot secure title insurance or lender's interest in land rights.
- **Valuation craters** when mineral rights, overriding royalties, or tribal land claims surface during title review.
- **Development stops** when a lessor demands renegotiation, threatening the project timeline and economics.

This chapter provides a framework for verifying land control—the disciplines and processes that separate projects you can safely acquire from projects that will strand you.

## Components of Land Control

Land control comprises several elements:

### 1. Lease or Easement Agreements

Most utility-scale renewable projects sit on leased land. The lease grants the developer (lessee) the right to use the land for a specified term, in exchange for rent.

Key lease terms include:

**Term:** How long the lease lasts (typically 20-40 years for renewable projects).

**Rent:** Annual or periodic payments, often escalating at a fixed percentage per year.

**Permitted Uses:** The lease specifies what the lessee can do (e.g., "construction, operation, and maintenance of a solar photovoltaic array").

**Renewal Options:** Many leases include renewal provisions, allowing the lessee to extend the term beyond the initial period.

**Assignment Restrictions:** Leases may restrict the lessee's ability to assign (transfer) the lease to a third party without lessor consent.

**Default Provisions:** Conditions under which the lessor can terminate the lease (e.g., non-payment of rent, abandonment of the project).

**Environmental & Regulatory Compliance:** Leases typically require the lessee to comply with laws and obtain permits.

**Restoration Obligations:** Upon project termination, the lessee must restore the land to agricultural or natural condition.

### 2. Lessor Identity & Authority

Before acquiring a project, confirm:

- **Who owns the land?** Title search and deed review.
- **Does the current lessee have authority to lease?** Confirm through power of attorney or corporate resolutions.
- **If the lessor is a couple, partnership, or corporation, are all owners party to the lease?** A lease signed by only one spouse may not bind the other; a partnership lease requires all partners.
- **Are there other liens or claims against the land?** Mortgage, judgment liens, tax liens?

Many deals have failed because a lessee represented that they held a valid lease from "John Smith" when the actual owner was "John Smith Sr." or a corporation named "Smith Family Trust LLC."

### 3. Land Area & Boundaries

A lease or easement must describe the land with sufficient clarity that a surveyor can locate and mark the boundaries.

Common boundary documentation includes:

- **Metes and bounds descriptions** (legal descriptions using distances and angles)
- **Acreage and survey maps** (showing the property outline)
- **GPS coordinates** (increasingly common for utility-scale projects)

During your verification, confirm:

- Does the survey map match the metes and bounds description?
- Are there boundary disputes with neighboring properties?
- Do any easements or rights-of-way cross the property? (e.g., electric lines, water pipes, county roads)
- Has the developer staked and surveyed the project area to confirm fit within the leased land?

### 4. Third-Party Consents

Often, multiple parties have claims to or interests in the land. These may include:

**Mortgage holders:** If the lessor has mortgaged the property, the lender's consent may be required for lease assignment.

**Other lessees or easement holders:** Perhaps a cell tower, electric line, or agricultural operation has prior rights.

**Tribal or conservation easements:** Some lands are subject to conservation restrictions or tribal co-management.

**Governmental interests:** Grazing permits, mineral leases, or water rights held by third parties may affect land use.

Before closing, secure **consents or non-disturbance agreements (NDAs)** from all third parties with material claims.

6.1 Case Study: The Lessor Consent Surprise

A renewable energy company was in the final stages of closing a 100 MW solar acquisition. Due diligence had been completed; financing was in place; the purchase agreement was signed.

Four days before closing, the buyer’s counsel discovered that the solar lease contained an assignment restriction requiring the lessor’s written consent for transfer of the lease to a new operator. The previous developer had obtained a written consent in 2015 when the project changed hands, but that consent explicitly stated it was “non-transferable” and “binding only on the original buyer.”

Because of the non-transferable language, the buyer could not rely on the 2015 consent. It needed a fresh consent from the lessor.

The lessor, learning of the buyer’s desperation and the imminent closing, demanded an additional $500,000 as a condition of consenting. The buyer’s financing did not accommodate such adjustments; the financing was tight and any price increase would breach debt covenants.

The deal closed, but at the last minute, with the buyer’s counsel negotiating the lessor down to $250,000 in additional rent, paid as an upfront lump sum. The buyer recovered some of this through renegotiation of other deal terms, but the transaction was materially impaired.

Lesson: Assignment restrictions are not mere formalities. A thorough review of existing consents—and verification that they actually permit subsequent transfers—is essential. Many deals have failed because a lessee, when pressed to provide proof of lessor consent to assignment, could not produce valid documentation.

6.2 Land Control Risks in M&A Contexts

When acquiring an existing project from another developer, additional land control risks emerge:

Non-Assignable Leases: The current developer’s lease may not permit assignment without breaking the lease or triggering a buyout.

Lessor Disputes: The lessor may claim the current developer breached the lease (e.g., failure to pay rent, unauthorized construction), giving the lessor grounds to terminate.

Sublease Conflicts: Some projects are subleased; if a sublessor goes bankrupt, the head lessee could reclaim the property.

Regulatory Changes: Zoning changes, conservation designations, or new regulations may restrict the lessor’s willingness to consent to assignment.

Lessor Death: If the lessor is an individual and passes away, the lease may be subject to estate disputes, creditor claims, or loss of authority by the lessee.

6.3 Best Practices for Land Control Verification

  1. Start Early: Land verification should begin during preliminary due diligence, before signing a purchase agreement. Do not wait until final due diligence.

  2. Engage Specialists: Use title attorneys, surveyors, and real estate brokers experienced in renewable energy. Their expertise in identifying and resolving land issues is invaluable.

  3. Document Everything: Maintain a comprehensive file of all lease agreements, amendments, consents, surveys, and correspondence. When issues arise, this documentation proves your diligence.

  4. Obtain Title Insurance: Title insurance protects the buyer if defects in lessor’s title surface after closing. Many lenders require title insurance as a condition of financing.

  5. Verify Assignability Early: Before signing a purchase agreement, confirm that the lease is assignable and that the lessor will consent to assignment. Do not assume.

  6. Secure Consents in Writing: All third-party consents must be in writing and specific to the transaction at hand. Oral consents are unenforceable.

  7. Site Walk: Always conduct a physical site walk with the lessor and surveyor to confirm boundaries and resolve any discrepancies before closing.

  8. Address Issues Immediately: If due diligence uncovers land control issues (boundary discrepancies, title defects, consent failures), address them immediately. Do not hope they will resolve themselves.

6.4 Key Takeaways

  • Land control is the foundation of every renewable energy project. Inadequate verification is a leading cause of M&A deal failure.
  • Land control comprises multiple elements: valid lease or easement, clear lessor identity and authority, precise boundaries, and third-party consents.
  • A disciplined land verification process—documentation review, title search, survey, lessor verification, third-party consents, and site walk—minimizes risk.
  • Many deals fail at closing because lessor consent was not obtained, or because the consent obtained did not permit subsequent transfer.
  • Engaging experienced specialists (title attorneys, surveyors, real estate brokers) and starting the verification early are essential best practices.
