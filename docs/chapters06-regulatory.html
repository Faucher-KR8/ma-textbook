Chapter 6: Regulatory and Permitting Risk – Navigating the Approval Gauntlet

Why Permitting Risk Is Different

Permitting risk is unlike other due diligence categories. Contamination either exists or it does not. Title is either clear or it is not. Permitting risk is dynamic—it can change based on who is in office, what community groups organize, and whether an agency has a staff backlog.

A wind project in the Mountain West had all permits in hand. The project was construction-ready. Three weeks before the notice to proceed, a neighboring landowner filed a federal lawsuit challenging the Bureau of Land Management’s environmental review. Construction stopped. Litigation lasted 22 months. The developer’s tax equity investors walked.

Permitting risk is not just a pre-closing concern—it can materialize at any stage of the project lifecycle.

This chapter explains the permitting landscape for utility-scale renewable energy, the most common permitting risks in M&A transactions, and how to assess and manage them.

The Permitting Landscape

Utility-scale renewable energy projects require permits from multiple jurisdictions simultaneously:

Federal Permits

Bureau of Land Management (BLM): Required for projects sited on federal land. BLM requires a right-of-way grant, which involves a NEPA environmental review (Environmental Assessment or Environmental Impact Statement).

Army Corps of Engineers (ACOE): Required for any fill of Waters of the United States, including wetlands (Section 404 permit).

U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS): Biological opinion required if any federal nexus exists and listed species are present.

Federal Aviation Administration (FAA): Required for any structure over 200 feet AGL (wind turbines). FAA review can restrict turbine height or placement near airports or military airspace.

Federal Communications Commission (FCC): Microwave communications corridors can conflict with wind turbine siting.

State Permits

State permitting requirements vary significantly. Key state permits include:

  • Certificate of Public Convenience and Necessity (CPCN) or equivalent siting certificate for large generation facilities
  • State environmental review (California CEQA, New York SEQRA, etc.)
  • Air quality permits for construction dust and equipment emissions
  • Stormwater permits (NPDES) for construction activity
  • Water rights permits in western states

Local Permits

Local permitting is increasingly the most challenging approval pathway:

  • Conditional Use Permits (CUPs) or Special Use Permits (SUPs)
  • Building permits for foundations, electrical, and structural work
  • Road use agreements for construction vehicle access
  • Decommissioning bonds required by many counties

Key Permitting Risk Categories in M&A

1. Permit Transferability

When a project is acquired, permits issued in the seller’s name must transfer to the buyer. Not all permits transfer automatically:

  • Most state and local permits require notification and formal transfer approval.
  • FAA determinations are site-specific and do not need to transfer—but if project modifications are required, new FAA filings are needed.
  • Water rights in western states may require formal transfer proceedings.
  • BLM right-of-way grants require BLM approval for assignment.

Failure to transfer permits before closing can result in the buyer operating without valid approvals.

2. Permit Conditions

Permits are rarely unconditional. Common permit conditions include:

  • Pre-construction surveys: Biological surveys required within a specified time window before construction.
  • Construction windows: Restrictions on when construction may occur (e.g., no construction during raptor nesting season, March–July).
  • Noise limits: Operational noise restrictions at project boundaries.
  • Decommissioning requirements: Financial assurance for decommissioning.
  • Shadow flicker limits: Restrictions on wind turbine operation to limit shadow flicker impact on neighboring properties.

Permit conditions must be reviewed carefully—conditions the seller accepted may be costly or operationally burdensome for the buyer.

3. Appeal Windows and Litigation

Permits may be appealed by project opponents:

  • Administrative appeals to state or local agencies delay project effectiveness.
  • Judicial review under NEPA or state equivalents can halt projects for years.
  • Intervenor status in utility regulatory proceedings can add months to timelines.

When reviewing permits, confirm whether appeal windows are still open and whether any appeals have been filed.

4. Local Opposition

Community opposition has become one of the most significant drivers of project failure. Opposition can result in:

  • Permit denial by local boards
  • Rezoning that eliminates renewable energy as a permitted use
  • Referendum campaigns that overturn local approvals
  • “Energy Exclusion Zones” adopted by some counties that ban utility-scale projects

Due diligence should include a community relations assessment—interviews with local officials, review of public meeting minutes, and monitoring of social media and local press.

Interconnection and Transmission Risk

Interconnection risk is frequently underweighted in M&A transactions:

Queue Position: Projects hold a position in the utility’s interconnection queue. Queue positions have value and can be lost if milestones are missed. Confirm the project’s queue position and whether any deadlines are approaching.

Network Upgrade Costs: Interconnection studies may require the project to fund transmission network upgrades costing millions of dollars. Confirm what upgrade costs have been assessed and whether they are final.

Interconnection Agreement: Confirm the interconnection agreement is executed, the deposit is current, and the agreement is assignable.

Transmission Capacity: In congested regions, even a connected project may face significant curtailment risk if transmission capacity is inadequate.

Case Study: The County Moratorium

A solar developer held a fully permitted 150 MW project in a rural Midwest county. The conditional use permit was valid, all state approvals were in hand, and construction financing was committed.

Six weeks before the buyer closed, the county commission voted to impose a 12-month moratorium on new solar projects and ordered a “review” of existing permits—including the seller’s conditional use permit.

The buyer’s lenders withdrew financing commitments pending resolution of the county’s legal authority to revoke an issued permit. Litigation lasted 18 months. The project ultimately prevailed, but at a cost of $2.1 million in legal fees and a 22-month construction delay.

Lesson: Local political risk must be monitored through closing—not just at initial due diligence. Include a closing condition requiring no material adverse change in permit status.

Best Practices for Permitting Due Diligence

  1. Obtain and Review All Permits: Do not rely on a permit summary. Obtain and read every permit, including all conditions and attachments.

  2. Confirm Transfer Requirements: Identify every permit that requires formal transfer or notification and build transfer timelines into the closing schedule.

  3. Check Appeal Status: Confirm whether appeal windows are open and whether any appeals have been filed against project approvals.

  4. Assess Interconnection Queue: Review queue position, milestone deadlines, and network upgrade cost allocations.

  5. Review Community Relations: Understand the local political environment. Meet with key officials or commission staff before closing.

  6. Include Permit Reps and Warranties: Require seller representations that all permits are valid, in good standing, and not subject to appeal.

Key Takeaways

  • Renewable energy projects require permits from federal, state, and local agencies simultaneously. Each layer carries independent risk.
  • Permit transfer is not automatic—many permits require formal assignment procedures at or before closing.
  • Appeal windows and litigation risk survive permit issuance and must be monitored through closing.
  • Interconnection risk (queue position, network upgrade costs, curtailment) is a material financial risk in M&A transactions.
  • Local opposition has become a leading cause of project failure in utility-scale renewable energy development.
